## 1. What is at stake

### Housing prices and rent

- Prices are falling, slowly, for the first time in years. The Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) home price index for April–May 2026 was 2.0% lower than a year earlier; new apartments were down 3.9%. Month on month, prices fell 1.0%, the biggest drop in eight years. [JPost 2026-07-16, https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/banking-and-finance/article-902755; INN 2026-07-15, https://www.israelnationalnews.com/news/430223; ToI headline "Housing snapshot August 2026: Home prices fall 1%, largest drop in eight years", https://www.timesofisrael.com/housing-snapshot-august-2026-home-prices-fall-1-largest-drop-in-eight-years/ (headline only)]
- The CBS called this "one of the periods in the past decade in which annual declines were recorded, following drops in 2018 and the second half of 2023." [INN, same URL]
- Regional split, April–May 2026, year on year: Central District −3.2%, Haifa −2.6%, Tel Aviv −2.5%, South −0.5%; North +1.4%, Jerusalem +0.3%. [INN, same URL]
- Latest release opened (CBS May–June 2026, published Sept 4): +0.1% on the previous two months, −1.5% year on year. "Prices have declined in eight out of the last 12 months." Reasons given: high interest rates, unsold new inventory, a shekel about 10% stronger against the dollar (NIS 2.99). Transactions in June 2026 were up 50% on June 2025. [ToI 2026-09-04, https://www.timesofisrael.com/housing-snapshot-september-2026-prices-inch-upward-after-sharp-decline/]
- Rent is going the other way. In the CBS release for April–May 2026, rent for tenants who renewed a lease rose 2.6% year on year; for new tenants, "a sharp jump of 6.6% was registered." [JPost 2026-07-16, URL above]
- Bank of Israel, 2025 full year: home prices −0.9%; about 80,000 housing starts; 7,400 housing units damaged by the war as of October 2025. [Bank of Israel Annual Report 2025, March 2026, https://www.boi.org.il/en/communication-and-publications/regular-publications/bank-of-israel-annual-report/bank-of-israel-annual-report-2025-1/]
- Long-run context: a Haaretz piece (2026-02-05) is headlined "Housing Up 90%, Groceries Up 60%: For Israel, a Terrible 20 Years of Price Rises." Paywalled; headline only; figures not verified. [https://www.haaretz.com/israel-news/2026-02-05/ty-article-magazine/.premium/housing-up-90-groceries-up-60-for-israel-a-terrible-20-years-of-price-rises/0000019c-2df0-d663-a39c-3dfac35e0000 (not opened)]
- Affordability: Yaron Zelekha (Reservists list no. 2) says an apartment costs about 170 average salaries. Class doc 11d; a campaign claim, not a CBS figure. (not opened by me)

What is worth noticing: a small fall in prices after a 20-year rise does not make housing affordable. Rents rising 6.6% for new tenants hits young people and renters hardest, and they are the people the opposition is courting.

### Food and other prices compared with the OECD

- OECD, June 2024 data: food and non-alcoholic drink prices in Israel were 52% above the average of 38 OECD countries, second only to South Korea. Bread and grains +49%; dairy and eggs +64%; meat +64%; fruit and vegetables +25%. Cars +52% (highest in the OECD). Telecom −30%, the one category cheaper than average. [ToI 2024-07-02, https://www.timesofisrael.com/food-and-beverage-prices-in-israel-52-higher-than-oecd-average-report/]
- The OECD's 2025 Economic Survey of Israel has a chapter titled "Addressing the high cost of living," which names trade barriers and tariffs and calls for steps to "durably lower consumer prices." I could not read the chapter's figures through the fetch tool. [https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-israel-2025_d6dd02bc-en/full-report/addressing-the-high-cost-of-living_bfe408a2.html]
- Inflation itself is not the problem now: CPI 2.6% in 2025 (Bank of Israel); 1.9% year on year in May 2026 (Moody's summary via Ynet). The complaint is the price level, not the rate of rise. [BoI report URL above; Ynet 2026-07-15, https://www.ynetnews.com/business/article/rkqzvq4vfe]
- Household strain (Time, 2026-07-13): one-third of households spend more than they earn; overdraft rates near 13%; about half the self-employed report earning less than before the war. [https://time.com/article/2026/07/13/israel-war-economy-cost-ordinary-israelis/] Time's sourcing for each figure is not given in what I could read; treat as press figures.

### The 2025 and 2026 budgets

- 2026 budget passed the Knesset March 30, 2026, 62–55, the day before the deadline that would have triggered automatic elections. [ToI 2026-03-30, https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-approves-2026-budget-israels-largest-ever-sending-billions-to-haredi-institutions/]
- Size: formal spending cap NIS 699 billion; about NIS 850.6 billion counting development budgets and other mechanisms. Defense NIS 143–144 billion, up from NIS 112 billion in the cabinet draft. Education NIS 97 billion, Health NIS 63 billion. "A broad cut of more than 3% across civilian ministries." Debt servicing NIS 151.8 billion. One-time bank tax NIS 3 billion. A proposed vacant-land tax and a dairy reform were dropped. Deficit target 4.9% of GDP, raised from 3.9%. [Ynet 2026-03-30, https://www.ynetnews.com/business/article/bjcpvkws11x; ToI same date]
- Quotes (both from ToI 2026-03-30): Finance Minister Smotrich said the budget "takes care of everyone and fights the cost of living." Opposition leader Lapid: "This isn't a budget — it's a robbery."
- The cabinet approved the draft only after a fight over defense spending; the March 2026 defense approval came "as Iran war rages" (Breaking Defense headline, https://breakingdefense.com/2026/03/israel-approves-45b-defense-budget-as-iran-war-rages/, not opened).
- 2025 budget: passed March 2025. I did not open a primary page on it in this session; figures left out. (See Stale or missing.)

### War costs, the deficit and debt

- Bank of Israel: war-related costs 2023–2026 about NIS 350 billion; NIS 116 billion of war spending in 2025 alone; cumulative output loss since the war began 8.6% of a year's GDP (NIS 177 billion). GDP grew 2.9% in 2025 after 1.0% in 2024. Unemployment averaged 3.0%. [BoI Annual Report 2025]
- Deficit 4.7% of GDP in 2025. Debt 68.5% of GDP at end-2025, up from 67.6% (end-2024) and 60.5% in early 2023. [BoI Annual Report; JPost early Oct 2026 (date not shown), https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-910242]
- Defense spending: about 4.5% of GDP before the war, about 8% in 2025, about 6% expected in 2026. [JPost article-910242]
- Moody's (July 2026 summary): 2026 central-government deficit 5.3% of GDP (above the 4.9% target), 4.4% in 2027; debt to stabilize near 70%; growth 3.7% in 2026 and 5% in 2027 "contingent on ceasefires." [Ynet 2026-07-15]
- Economist voices (JPost, early Oct 2026): Adrian Filut says war spending left "very little fiscal room"; Elise Brezis: "When you look at that compared to many OECD countries, our situation is not so bad." Both name Haredi and Arab labor-market integration as the long-term priority.

### The reservist economic burden

- Finance Ministry (Aug 2026): a month of reserve duty costs the economy on average NIS 53,000 per reservist (older reservists NIS 68,000; young NIS 39,000), against NIS 26,000 for a regular conscript. Replacing 10,000 reservists with conscripts would save NIS 2.7 billion a year. "Non-Haredi Jewish men account for only 36% of all employees, but make up 83% of those absent from work due to reserve duty." [INN 2026-08-25, https://www.israelnationalnews.com/news/432226]
- IDI (Nov 2024, data to July 2024): reserve duty took 5% of all working hours in October 2023 (7% of men's hours, 1% of women's); about 1% by July 2024 (about 34,000 workers a month), against under 0.1% before the war. At a 1% level the annual cost is about NIS 7.9 billion. Self-employed absences peaked at 2.8% (about 14,000 people). Compensation spending NIS 8.2 billion in 2023 against NIS 1.6 billion a year before. [IDI, https://en.idi.org.il/articles/56943]
- Bank of Israel Governor Amir Yaron (Dec 2025): relying on repeated reserve duty as "a substitute for expanding compulsory service" is "costly both from a budget perspective and due to the disruption to the labor market." [ToI 2025-12-04, https://www.timesofisrael.com/bank-of-israel-governor-warns-funding-yeshiva-students-draft-exemptions-threatens-growth/]
- Reservists' Wives Forum: 20,000 women representing about 100,000 families (Time 2026-07-13).

This is where the economy issue and the draft issue become the same issue. The people doing reserve duty are mostly non-Haredi Jewish men, who are also the core of the tax base.

### Haredi and Arab labor-force participation

- Haredi men's employment: 52% (2015), 53.1% (2022), 53% (2025) — "a lost decade." Under-40 Haredi men: 47.3% in 2025, lower than a decade earlier. Government target: 65% by 2030; the Labor Ministry report says the figure "sparks pessimism regarding compliance." [JPost opinion, article-901982, http://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/opinion/article-901982, citing the Labor Ministry's 2025 labor-market report; article date not shown in what I read]
- IDI Statistical Report on Haredi Society (Dec 2025): Haredi men 53% employed, Haredi women 81%; Haredim 14.3% of the population (about 1.45 million); 57% aged 19 or under; fertility 6.5. Haredi men earn on average NIS 9,929 a month, 49% of non-Haredi Jewish men's income. 33% of Haredi families below the poverty line. "The data reflects a decade of stagnation and even a decline in the integration of ultra-Orthodox men." [Media Line 2025-12-14, https://themedialine.org/headlines/israel-democracy-institute-report-finds-stalled-integration-of-ultra-orthodox-men-as-community-growth-accelerates/]
- Taub Center, A Picture of the Nation 2026 (June 29, 2026): Arab men 77% employed (early 2025), down from a 79% peak in 2023; Arab women 49% (H1 2025); non-Haredi Jewish men about 87%. Haredi men's employment in 2025 fell below 2023. "If these trends continue, the employment rate of Arab women is expected to surpass that of Haredi men." Average adult skills in Israel are about 0.3 standard deviations below the OECD average, a full standard deviation in the Arab population. [https://www.taubcenter.org.il/en/pr/pon-2026/]
- Bank of Israel Governor Yaron (Dec 2025) on yeshiva funding: such budgets "create incentives for non-participation in the labor market and for not acquiring education" and "harm long-term growth and living standards, and their impact will increase over time." About 80,000 Haredi men aged 18–24 eligible for service had not enlisted. [ToI 2025-12-04]
- Older context: in May 2023, 280 economists warned the coalition's Haredi allocations could turn Israel "from an advanced and prosperous country to a backward country." [ToI 2023-05-21, https://www.timesofisrael.com/backward-country-economists-warn-government-over-haredi-budget-allocations/] Over two years old; use only as background.

**The "two economies" argument.** The brief names the Taub Center. What I could confirm: Taub's 2026 report does not use the phrase "two economies" in its press summary. The best-known version of the argument — a small, highly productive, mostly secular slice of the population (high-tech, medicine, academia) carries the tax base and the army, while a fast-growing share of children get an education that will not prepare them for that economy — is made by Dan Ben-David, now of the Shoresh Institution (he was earlier at Taub). I could not open a 2025–26 Shoresh page in this session. The Israel Innovation Authority data below back the "few carry the many" half of the argument with official figures.

### Tech and the "relocation" wave

- High-tech (Israel Innovation Authority, Sept 2025): 17% of GDP; 57% of exports in H1 2025, the highest share ever; 403,000 workers, 11.5% of the workforce. But output "stagnant for two years," R&D jobs down 6.5%, new startups about 500 in 2024 against 1,000+ a decade earlier. [https://innovationisrael.org.il/en/press_release/innovation-report-2025/]
- High-tech paid 36% of all income tax in 2021; 57% of 2021 income tax came from Jewish men in central Israel and Tel Aviv. [IIA, Aug 2024, https://innovationisrael.org.il/en/press_release/high-tech-contribution-2-state-revenues/] Data are from 2021 — older than two years.
- Emigration: CBS — 69,300 residents left in 2025 and 19,000 returned; net −20,000 Israelis (population still grew 1.1%). [Ynet, https://www.ynetnews.com/article/r1zatkmnzg] Tel Aviv University researchers (Ater, Bergman, Zamir): about 49,000 left for 12+ months in each of 2023 and 2024, an estimated 45,000–50,000 in 2025; "roughly 50,000 more people" left than arrived across 2024–25. "These figures on their own do not yet pose a threat to Israel's resilience." [ToI 2026-08-03, https://www.timesofisrael.com/record-israeli-emigration-continued-for-third-consecutive-year-in-2025-study-finds/]
- Who leaves: a Tax Authority study (Aug 2026) found emigration of high-tech workers up about 150% and of health workers more than doubled since 2022; the top income decile's emigration rate rose from 0.3% to over 0.5%; emigrants in that decile account for about 86% of emigrants' income tax; estimated annual tax loss about NIS 700 million. [JPost 2026-08-09, https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/banking-and-finance/article-904992]
- Reasons: research by Lilach Lev Ari cited by ToI says leavers point to government policy, democratic concerns and security, not mainly economics.

- Cabinet, March 10, 2026: NIS 5 billion in coalition funds, of which NIS 2.2 billion (53%) to Haredi purposes, including NIS 1.56 billion for yeshivas and kollels. Analyst Aviad Houminer-Rosenblum called it "a terrible, awful decision." [ToI, https://www.timesofisrael.com/cabinet-okays-1-6-billion-in-coalition-funds-including-715-million-for-ultra-orthodox/]
- JPost (March 22, 2026) gives NIS 6 billion, "a record level of coalition funds" (MK Vladimir Beliak, Yesh Atid). [https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-890836] See disagreements.

## 4. How an American reader is likely to misread this

1. **"It's the economy, stupid" does not hold the way it does in the US.** In American elections the economy is usually the top issue and drives swing voters. In Israel the economy ties security among Jews, and voting runs mainly along identity and security lines (bloc, religiosity, attitude to Netanyahu). The IDI data show the economy as the top factor only for Center voters and Arab citizens. Israelis can tell a pollster prices are their biggest worry and still vote on security or the draft.
2. **Left/right is not about economics.** In Israel "right" and "left" mean positions on security, territory and the courts. Likud's own voters are often poorer and more Mizrahi; the secular, high-income Tel Aviv vote goes to the center-left. Several right-wing lists campaign for welfare (Shas), and the most market-reform list (Reservists–Zelekha) sits in the anti-Netanyahu camp.
3. **The Haredi employment story is the draft story.** A Haredi man who registers as a full-time yeshiva student keeps his draft deferral only while he is not working. So the deferral and the low employment rate are one policy. (This mechanism is background knowledge, not sourced in this record; the fact-check pass needs a primary source for it, e.g. IDI's draft explainer.) An American reading "53% of Haredi men work" may assume a jobs-training problem; in Israel it is tied to the draft law, the stipends, and the coalition funds. The Finance Ministry's 36% / 83% reservist figure is the same point from the other side.
4. **"Cost of living" is about cartels and imports more than inflation.** Inflation is near 2%. The complaint is the price level (52% above OECD on food), which Israeli parties blame on concentrated importers, food producers and import rules. Promises are about breaking monopolies, not about the central bank.
5. **A booming stock market and a strained household can both be true.** Tel Aviv's index rose 52% in 2025 (Time); the tech sector is 57% of exports. Neither tells you much about rent for a reservist's family in the north.
6. **"Coalition funds" are a normal tool, not a scandal word in Israel by itself.** Every coalition hands out sectoral money as the price of votes on the budget. The 2026 controversy is about size, timing in wartime, and that much of it went to yeshivas whose students are not drafted.
