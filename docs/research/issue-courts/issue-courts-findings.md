## 1. What is at stake

### No constitution; Basic Laws instead
- Israel has no single written constitution. On June 13, 1950 the First Knesset adopted the Harari Decision: instead of one constitution, the Knesset's Constitution, Law and Justice Committee would write separate "Basic Laws," to be combined into a constitution later. That combining never happened. There are now 14 Basic Laws. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Laws_of_Israel)
- Most Basic Laws can be passed or changed by a simple majority of those present. A few have entrenchment clauses (for example, 61 of 120 votes to change the electoral system). (same URL) The Knesset "can pass any law by a simple majority, even one that might arguably conflict with the Basic Laws of Israel, unless the basic law includes specific conditions for its modification." (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knesset)
- The Knesset is a single chamber ("the unicameral legislature of Israel"), and the government exists because it holds a majority there (it must win a confidence vote before taking office). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knesset) So in practice the governing coalition controls both the executive and the legislature. Israel also has no federal or state layer and no presidential veto with teeth. The courts — mainly the Supreme Court sitting as the High Court of Justice — and the government legal advisers headed by the attorney general are the main checks on a coalition. This is the core of the dispute: one side sees the court as the only brake, the other sees an unelected brake with no brake on itself.

### The 1990s "constitutional revolution"
- In 1992 the Knesset passed Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty (vote 32–21) and Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation (23–0). Both said rights could be limited only "by a law befitting the values of the State of Israel." (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Laws_of_Israel) The small vote count for a law that later became the basis for striking down legislation is a recurring argument on the right.
- In the 1995 Bank Mizrahi decision the Supreme Court held that the Basic Laws form "a written constitution that the courts had the power to uphold via judicial review," so an ordinary law that violates them can be voided. Chief Justice Aharon Barak called this a "constitutional revolution." (same URL)
- Under Barak the court also widened who may petition (standing) and what questions courts may decide (justiciability), and used a "reasonableness" test on government decisions. Not verified from a primary source in this pass; noted in the Stale section.

### The 2023 overhaul package
Announced by Justice Minister Yariv Levin in January 2023, four main parts (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Israeli_judicial_reform):
1. **Reasonableness.** Bar courts from striking government and ministerial decisions as "unreasonable."
2. **Judicial Selection Committee.** Enlarge it from 9 to 11 and give the government a built-in majority. Under the old rules, a Supreme Court appointment needs 7 of 9 votes, and the committee is 3 justices, 2 ministers, 2 MKs and 2 Israel Bar Association representatives. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Israel)
3. **Override clause.** Let the Knesset re-enact a law the court struck down with 61 votes, if the ruling was not unanimous.
4. **Legal advisers.** Turn ministry legal advisers into political appointees whose advice is not binding.

What happened:
- Mass protests and a general strike followed; Netanyahu paused the legislation on March 27, 2023. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Israeli_judicial_reform; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Israeli_legislative_election)
- On April 3, 2023 Levin was quoted from an earlier Channel 14 interview conceding the dangers of coalition control over all branches: "this cannot happen in a democratic country." (https://www.timesofisrael.com/levin-admits-original-judicial-appointments-bill-was-a-danger-to-democracy/)
- **Only the reasonableness piece passed**: July 24, 2023, 64–0 (opposition walked out). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Israeli_judicial_reform)

### January 1, 2024: the court strikes the reasonableness law
- A full 15-justice panel struck down the reasonableness amendment 8–7, the first time the court voided a Basic Law or an amendment to one. By 12–3 it held that the court does have authority to review Basic Laws in extreme cases. The majority said the change would cause "severe and unprecedented harm to the core characteristics of Israel as a democratic state." (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Israeli_judicial_reform; https://www.npr.org/2024/01/01/1222400537/israel-supreme-court-ruling-judiciary-changes — the NPR summary tool misreported the date; the URL and Wikipedia give Jan 1, 2024)
- Point to notice: the 12–3 holding matters more than the 8–7 outcome. It is the court claiming the last word over the Knesset's constitution-making power, which is exactly what the right objects to.

### 2025: the selection law passes, effective after the election
- January 9–10, 2025: Levin and Gideon Sa'ar announced a narrower plan (selection panel, a higher bar for passing Basic Laws, a higher bar for courts striking laws). Levin: "a real fundamental change, and on the other hand is deliberate and balanced." The Bar Association head called it a "deceptive and dangerous proposal to implement the principles of the coup." (https://forward.com/fast-forward/687526/israels-judicial-overhaul-is-back-so-are-the-protests)
- **March 27, 2025: the Knesset passed the amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary changing the selection committee, 67 in favor.** The opposition boycotted the vote. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Israeli_judicial_reform)
- What it does (https://www.timesofisrael.com/regime-change-high-court-tears-into-coalition-bid-to-control-judicial-appointments/): the committee stays at nine, but the two Bar Association seats go to two lawyers chosen one by the coalition and one by the opposition. Remaining: two ministers, one coalition MK, one opposition MK, three justices. Lower-court appointments need agreement from coalition, opposition and court representatives. Supreme Court justices can be appointed without the justices' consent. If there is no agreement within a year, coalition and opposition each nominate three candidates and the other side picks — the deadlock mechanism. It takes effect when the next Knesset convenes, i.e. after the October 27, 2026 election.
- Supreme Court seats: 15 total; 4 currently vacant (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Israel); JTA reports six more justices reach mandatory retirement (70) within three years, so the next government could fill about two-thirds of the court (https://www.jta.org/2026/09/18/israel/judicial-reform-2-0-how-netanyahus-plans-to-reshape-the-courts-could-affect-israels-democracy).
- Court challenge: on Dec 2, 2025 the court said all eleven sitting justices would hear the petitions (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Israeli_judicial_reform). Hearing June 21, 2026: President Isaac Amit said the law would "implant a political chip" in judges; Justice Yechiel Kasher: "The coalition will put up three candidates who will be the most extreme…the opposition will do the same." Conservative Justice Alex Stein argued judges would bend rulings to win promotion. MK Simcha Rothman called justices ruling on Basic Law amendments "criminals." ToI's reading: nine of eleven justices were critical. (ToI URL above) Supplementary briefs in late July 2026; the Knesset argued "a Basic Law should not be invalidated merely because the court considers the previous system preferable." (https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-903801, July 27, 2026) **No ruling found as of Oct 5, 2026.**

### Supreme Court president and the committee freeze
- After Esther Hayut retired on Oct 15, 2023, the court went more than 16 months without a permanent president. Isaac Amit was selected in January 2025 and took office Feb 13, 2025. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Supreme_Court_of_Israel) Levin's refusal to recognize Amit is widely reported but was not verified from a page I opened.
- Levin did not convene the selection committee for regular appointments from January 2025. On May 31, 2026 a three-justice panel unanimously ordered him to convene it; there were 51 vacancies in magistrate and district courts. The panel: "The difficulty in the minister's position is even more acute since the [tight] timetable is the result of his own conduct." Levin called it "an apparently illegal decision." (https://www.timesofisrael.com/high-court-orders-justice-minister-to-convene-judicial-selection-committee/) The committee met June 28, 2026 and chose 68 judges and registrars; Amit: "After a long period of preparation and anticipation, I am pleased to announce that the committee convened today and selected dozens of judges." (https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-900747)

### The attorney general fight
- Gali Baharav-Miara was appointed Feb 7, 2022. Cabinet "no confidence" vote March 23, 2025; in June 2025 the government replaced the professional dismissal committee with a ministers' committee. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gali_Baharav-Miara)
- Aug 4, 2025: the government voted unanimously to fire her, accusing her of "serially thwarting its will over policy, appointments and legislation." Within an hour Justice Noam Sohlberg (a conservative) froze the dismissal. She called the move "unlawful." (https://www.timesofisrael.com/high-court-freezes-dismissal-of-attorney-general-after-government-votes-to-fire-her/)
- Dec 14, 2025: a seven-justice panel, including Sohlberg and David Mintz (both counted as conservatives), annulled the firing 7–0; the procedural change was adopted "hastily" and "without an appropriate factual or legal basis." Levin: "The government must totally reject this unlawful intervention into its authority." Minister Shlomo Karhi urged the government to "block her entry to government offices." (https://www.timesofisrael.com/high-court-annuls-firing-of-attorney-general-cabinet-ministers-urge-defiance-of-ruling/)
- Bill to split the AG's job: as introduced Oct 2025, three politically appointed posts and an end to binding AG opinions (https://www.timesofisrael.com/coalition-set-to-advance-bill-that-would-gut-attorney-general-position/). A version splitting it into an attorney general and a government-appointed prosecutor general — who could revisit Netanyahu's indictments — passed first reading 65–47 on June 2, 2026 (https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-898054). On June 29 it was reported unlikely to pass before dissolution (https://www.israelnationalnews.com/news/429346). The Knesset dissolved July 17, 2026. **I found no report that it passed; final status not confirmed.**
- Context the page must carry: the AG's office is prosecuting Netanyahu (trial since May 2020; pardon request Nov 30, 2025; Herzog deferred April 2026 — class doc 11a). Opponents say the overhaul serves his trial; supporters say the AG acted as an unelected veto on policy. Both claims belong on the page as claims.

### The Shin Bet chief affair
- March 20, 2025: cabinet voted to fire Shin Bet chief Ronen Bar; Netanyahu said he had "lost trust." The court froze it March 21. In an April 21 affidavit Bar said Netanyahu had demanded "personal loyalty" and "obedience to him and not to the Supreme Court." Bar resigned April 28, effective June 15, 2025. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronen_Bar)
- May 21, 2025: the court ruled the firing "unlawful," finding Netanyahu "was in a conflict of interest" because of the leaked-documents and "Qatargate" investigations involving his aides; no orders because Bar was leaving anyway. Netanyahu called it "disgraceful." (https://www.timesofisrael.com/high-court-rules-shin-bet-heads-dismissal-unlawful-pm-had-conflict-of-interest/)
- Successor Maj.-Gen. (res.) David Zini: approved Sept 30, 2025, in office Oct 5, 2025; a challenge was rejected 2–1 in December 2025. (https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-907513)

### Where things stand, October 2026
- Law on the books: the selection-committee law, effective when the 26th Knesset convenes, unless the court strikes it first. Ruling pending.
- Struck: reasonableness (2024). Never passed: override clause, legal-advisers law. AG-split bill: passed first reading, apparently not enacted.
- AG Baharav-Miara in office. Shin Bet led by Zini. Court president Amit. Four Supreme Court vacancies.
- The right's next-term plan, called "Law and Justice 2.0" by Smotrich (JTA, Sept 18, 2026, URL above): limit strike-downs, restrict standing, use the new appointment system. Levin, in Kan recordings (Aug 2026): "We'll wait for the next term, when two-thirds of the Supreme Court justices' seats become vacant. I told them… I won't need to carry out a reform. You'll simply go extinct." (JTA, same URL)
- The courts are inside this election too: on Oct 2, 2026 the Supreme Court reversed the Central Elections Committee's 18–5 votes barring the Joint List and Ra'am (class doc 11d; Al Jazeera 2026-10-02). On Oct 3 elections committee chair Sohlberg ordered a Ben-Gvir campaign video removed (class doc 11b).

## 4. How an American reader is likely to misread this

1. **Mapping it onto US Supreme Court fights.** American readers expect a conservative court and a liberal opposition, or a fight over abortion or guns. Israel's fight is over the court's *structure and power*, not its rulings on a culture-war docket. And the court has conservative justices (Sohlberg, Mintz, Stein) who joined the 7–0 AG ruling and criticized the selection law (ToI, URLs above).
2. **"Judicial review" means something different.** The US court reviews against a written, hard-to-amend constitution. Israel's court reviews against Basic Laws that a simple majority can pass — the 1992 dignity law passed 32–21. In 2024 the court went further and said it may review the Basic Laws themselves. Supporters of the overhaul argue that would be like the US court striking a constitutional amendment; opponents answer that a Basic Law in Israel is not like a US amendment, because it takes no supermajority, no second chamber and no states to ratify.
3. **Missing the absent checks.** No Senate, no states, no presidential veto, no staggered elections, no written constitution — and the government sits on top of its own Knesset majority. That is why the courts and the attorney general carry so much weight, and why the fight is so fierce on both sides.
4. **Appointments work differently.** No presidential nomination and Senate confirmation, no life tenure: retirement at 70, and appointments made by a mixed committee in which the justices have had an effective veto (7 of 9). Both "the government should pick judges, as in America" and "judges pick judges" are half-truths.
5. **Reading it as a left–right fight only.** It lines up most closely with religiosity and with the coalition–opposition divide. Haredim and national religious Jews barely trust the court (3% and 19%); secular Jews mostly do (66%). Arab citizens are not reliably pro-court.
6. **Assuming it is the top election issue.** In the September 2026 IVI, only 18% of Jews named judicial reform as a main vote consideration — behind security, economy, the draft and religion-state. For most voters it is part of the larger question of whether to keep Netanyahu.
7. **Missing the trial.** American coverage often drops the Netanyahu prosecution, or makes it the whole story. Both sides frame the fight through it, and the page should say so neutrally.

## Source disagreements
- **Date of the selection law.** The ToI fetch summary said "passed in March 2026"; Wikipedia gives March 27, 2025 and JTA/LoC say "after the next election," consistent with 2025. Treat 2026 as a summarizer error, but confirm against the ToI page text before publishing.
- **Reasonableness ruling date.** The NPR summary tool gave Sept 11, 2023 (the hearing); the URL and Wikipedia give Jan 1, 2024. Use Jan 1, 2024.
- **Trust measures differ.** IDI (Jews 42%, May 2025; 4-point scale, phone+online) vs Reichman (total 43%, Jan 2026; online). They are close but not the same population or method; don't merge them into one series.
- **Amcha's judiciary position.** Class doc 11b: "no details"; Ynet Sept 22: specific proposals. Ynet is newer.
- **Supreme Court vacancies.** Wikipedia: 11 sitting, 4 vacant; JTA: "four justices retired… during the last government." Consistent; JTA's "two-thirds" assumes six more retirements in three years.
- **Arab views.** Arabs are more opposed to the overhaul (Jan 2025: 79%) yet more likely than Jews to say the court intervenes too much (55.5%). Both are IDI figures; IDI says it cannot explain this.
