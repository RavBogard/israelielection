## 1. Size, growth, geography

### How many people
The count depends on who is being counted: immigrants, immigrants plus Israeli-born children, or eligible voters. The sources don't agree, and they are counting different things.

- **About 1.25 million** Soviet and post-Soviet immigrants arrived from 1989 to mid-2023. That is about 17% of Israel's Jewish population and about 13% of the total population (Vladimir/Ze'ev Khanin, Bar-Ilan, *Riddle*, 2023-08-03, https://ridl.io/war-aliyah-of-russian-speaking-jews-to-israel-past-experience-and-new-surprises/). Khanin says about 14% of them later left Israel again.
- **About 1.3 million Russian speakers, ~15% of the population** (2022). This is the commonly cited figure (Wikipedia, "Russians in Israel," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russians_in_Israel — the underlying source was not opened; the same figure appears in *Eurasian Times* 2026-08-03, https://www.eurasiantimes.com/russians-hold-the-key-to-israels-pm-office-why-netanyahus-fate-hinges-on-the-putin-aliyah/). verified: false.
- **About 1 million eligible voters, "roughly 15%"** (Lazar Research via JPost, 2025-12-10, https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-879843). The article blurs "15% of voters" and "15% of the population."
- **770,000 eligible voters, 12% of the electorate, worth 15–16 seats**, as of 2019 (Zeev Hanin, cited by ToI 2019-07-26, https://www.timesofisrael.com/russians-are-coming-to-the-polls-israeli-politicians-are-finally-waking-up/).
- **Self-identification is much lower.** In IDI's Democracy Index 2025, only 5.3% of Jewish respondents named "FSU immigrant" as their origin group (p. 322, https://en.idi.org.il/media/30427/the-israeli-democracy-index-2025-full-english-version.pdf). Most of the Israeli-born second generation, and many immigrants, choose "Ashkenazi" or "mixed" instead. This is worth saying on the page: the group is large by birthplace and language, but a much smaller share of people treat it as their main identity.

### The 1990s wave
- Arrivals: 185,200 in 1990 and 147,800 in 1991, then 46,000–68,000 a year through 2000. The total for 1989–2006 was about 979,000 (Wikipedia table citing JVL and Haaretz, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990s_post-Soviet_aliyah; verified: false for the underlying figures).
- An earlier wave of more than 150,000 came in the 1970s. Avigdor Liberman came in 1978.

### The 2022–25 wave (Russia's invasion of Ukraine)
- From February 2022 to June 2023: 47,000+ arrived from Russia and 15,500 from Ukraine. That was about two-thirds of all new citizens in that period (Khanin 2023, link above).
- Immigration from the FSU by year (CBS via JVL, taken from class doc 06; verified: false because the JVL table did not load when opened):

| Year | From FSU | All countries |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 63,112 | 71,354 |
| 2023 | 39,490 | 46,101 |
| 2024 | 22,628 | 29,859 |
| 2025 | 11,018 | 19,894 |

- Russia alone: 19,535 in 2024 and 8,553 in 2025, down 56% (CBS, *Israel in Figures*, Rosh Hashana 2026, via JVL, https://jewishvirtuallibrary.org/latest-population-statistics-for-Israel). JVL's wording applies 8,553 to both "FSU" and "Russia," so that line is ambiguous.
- Total since February 2022: about 125,000 (*Eurasian Times*, no primary source given). Class doc 05 has ~80,000 from Russia plus ~20,000 from Ukraine and Belarus (JPost 2025-09-20). The figures differ by agency and definition.
- **Many of these arrivals have not stayed or are thinking of leaving.** IDI's "Stay or Go?" (fieldwork 2025-04-06 to 04-17, CI Marketing; published 2025-12-03) asked FSU immigrants who arrived 2014–2025. About 60% are considering moving to another country, and only 21% are considering going back to their country of origin. Among those aged 18–34 the figure is 77%, and among secular immigrants who are not Jewish it is 86%. For all Jews the figure is about a quarter (https://en.idi.org.il/articles/62287). The size of the FSU subsample was not given.

### Not recognized as Jewish by the Rabbinate
The brief's figure of "~300–400k" holds up as a range. Where a source falls in that range depends on its definition and date:
- "More than 300,000," about 30% of the 1989–2019 immigrants, were not Jewish under halacha (Khanin 2023).
- About 400,000 people living in Israel "are not considered Jewish by the Chief Rabbinate," most of them from the FSU (ToI 2019-01-04, https://www.timesofisrael.com/why-most-new-immigrants-to-israel-arent-considered-jewish/).
- Israelis registered as of "no religion": "roughly half a million" (Knesset Research and Information Center via ToI 2022-11-17, https://www.timesofisrael.com/data-shows-major-drop-in-immigrants-from-former-soviet-union-considered-jewish/). This category includes people who are not from the FSU, and it is not the same thing as "not recognized by the Rabbinate."
- The share of each year's FSU arrivals who are not Jewish under halacha rose from 6.8% in 1990 to 71.7% in 2020 (Knesset RIC, same ToI article). Another source gives 4% in 1990 and 53% in 2000 (Reinharz and DellaPergola via Wikipedia; verified: false). So the 2022–25 wave is mostly people eligible under the Law of Return's grandchild clause who are not Jewish under halacha. That means the 300–400k figure is now probably an undercount. No post-2022 total was found.
- Conversions run at about 2,000 Israeli citizens a year (Itim via ToI 2019). That is far below the community's natural growth (IDI, Yedidia Stern 2010, https://en.idi.org.il/articles/10170).
- What this means in practice: with no civil marriage in Israel, these citizens cannot marry a Jew inside the country. Stern (IDI 2010): "80% of the population of Israel is off-limits to them for the purpose of marriage."

### Where they live
CBS locality tables were not reachable: cbs.gov.il reset the connection every time, so share-of-locality figures could not be pulled. What can be shown:
- 1990s settlement went to Gush Dan, Haifa, Beersheba (which had the largest caravan site, 2,308 units), Petah Tikva and Ashdod (Wikipedia, verified: false).
- Nof HaGalil (Nazareth Illit until 2019) in 2024: 45,805 residents, 51.6% Jewish, 28.7% Arab, 19.7% "other" (CBS via Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nof_HaGalil; verified: false). The "other" category in a city like this is mostly FSU immigrants who are not Jewish under halacha (inference). Wikipedia also reports that the municipality "has not approved the construction of churches, mosques, or public Arabic-speaking schools."
- The CEC results (section 2) are a usable proxy for where the group is concentrated. Yisrael Beiteinu's share is highest in Nof HaGalil, Arad, Kiryat Yam, Karmiel, Ariel, Ma'alot-Tarshiha, Bat Yam, Ashkelon and Ashdod.

### Age, education, income
- The group is older. Former-USSR-born people are 35.8% of Holocaust survivors living in Israel (about 47,700 of 133,000) (CBS release, 2024-05-02, https://www.cbs.gov.il/he/mediarelease/doclib/2024/132/11_24_132e.pdf). This was the only CBS age figure found for the group.
- Education: about 60% of the 1989–90 arrivals had college degrees, and some 57,000 engineers and 12,000 doctors came (JPost via Wikipedia; verified: false). "One in four staff members at Israel's universities" are native Russian speakers (Wikipedia; verified: false).
- Income: in 1992, 56% were in the poorest third of the population. By 2010 that was down to 38%, with 27% in the richest third. By 2012 average wages had reached the level of native-born Jews (Haaretz/JPost via Wikipedia; verified: false).
- Missing: current CBS or Taub figures on wages, pensioner poverty, and second-generation education.

## 2. Voting pattern 2019–2022

### Group-level estimates (survey-based)
| Election | YB | Likud | Other | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2015 | 42% | 29% | Labor 6.3% | Hanin via ToI 2019 |
| Apr 2019 | 40.2% | 26.7% | Blue and White 15.1, Kulanu 5.8, Zehut 4, New Right 3.2, Labor 2.3, Shas 1.5 | Hanin via ToI 2019 |

What is worth noticing:
1. **YB peaked everywhere in September 2019.** That was the election right after Liberman refused to join Netanyahu over the draft law. The party has declined in every one of these localities in each election since.
2. **In the Russian-speaking strongholds, 2022 came in below April 2019** (Nof HaGalil −5.7 points, Karmiel −2.1, Ashkelon −2.5, Bat Yam −1.4, Ashdod −2.0). **But nationally, 2022 (4.48%) came in above April 2019 (4.01%).** This fits the party's base becoming less Russian-speaking and more general-secular, which is what the party itself has been aiming for (Wikipedia: support from the original demographic "is in decline"; IPF 2019). This is an inference from aggregate data, not a measured fact.
3. **Likud did not obviously gain in these localities.** Likud's share in them peaked in 2020 and in 2022 was at or below its April 2019 level (Ashdod 33.9 → 28.9; Ariel 43.7 → 40.6; Nof HaGalil 29.9 → 27.9). Locality data cannot show an age-based shift to Likud among younger immigrants. The only evidence for a Likud lean among newcomers is the Lazar poll's subsample (below).
4. **Turnout is low.** In 2022, turnout was 50.8% in Bat Yam, 52.8% in Nof HaGalil, 55.5% in Haifa and 55.6% in Kiryat Yam, against 70.6% nationally. Two things push these numbers down: eligible-voter rolls include people who have emigrated, and Nof HaGalil and Haifa have Arab populations with lower turnout. Riddle (2019) also reports below-average turnout among Russian speakers.
5. In Nof HaGalil, Arab parties took 25.6% of the 2022 vote. So YB's share of the Jewish-party vote there is about 20%.
6. Yesh Atid was strong in Haifa (26.3%), Karmiel (24.4%) and Arad (18.1%) in 2022. Religious Zionism was strong in Ariel (18.8%) and Beersheba (15.7%).

### 2026
- YB's national polling: 8–10 seats from January to June 2026 (Wikipedia polling table, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_2026_Israeli_legislative_election). Late-August polls: Kan 8, Channel 14 6, Zman 10, Channel 16 8, Channel 13 9. Late September to early October (class doc 11c): Maariv 9, Channel 13 9, Zman 10, Kan 9, Channel 14 7/6/6.
- Among the Russian-speaking group specifically, the only 2025–26 poll found is Lazar (n=663, published 2025-12-10; fieldwork date not given). YB 23% (about 3.5 seats), Bennett 17% (about 2.5 seats). **Among 2022–25 arrivals: "Likud's Yuli Edelstein" 37%, Liberman 32%.** Caveats:
  - The poll was led by Dr. Elina Bardach-Yalov, who was a Yisrael Beiteinu MK in 2021–22 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elina_Bardach-Yalov).
  - Edelstein was removed from the Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee in 2025 over the draft bill (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuli-Yoel_Edelstein). Wikipedia's 2026 election page calls Ze'ev Elkin "the only Russian speaker on [Likud's] list," named head of immigrant outreach on Aug 25 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Israeli_legislative_election). If Edelstein is not on Likud's 2026 list, the 37% figure no longer describes a real ballot choice. **Verify Edelstein's 2026 status before using this number.**
- A second study, by Dr. Hagay Elkayam-Shalem (600+ post-2022 arrivals, reported by *Eurasian Times* 2026-08-03), found 45% backing opposition parties and 13% backing coalition parties. Self-placement: 25% center-right, 30% centrist, 18% center-left, 27% left. The study itself was not opened (verified: false), and the self-placement list has no "right" category, which is odd. **It points in a different direction from Lazar** (see disagreements).
- YB's 2026 list: of the top ten, Liberman (b. Chișinău, arrived 1978), Yulia Malinovsky (b. Luhansk, arrived 1998) and Evgeny Sova (b. Pervomaisk, arrived 1997, formerly of Channel 9 Russian-language TV) are FSU-born. The rest include Rafi Ben Shitrit, Sharon Sharabi, Hamad Amar (Druze) and Dan Illouz (ex-Likud) (ToI 2026-09-06, https://www.timesofisrael.com/bennett-liberman-unveil-byachad-yisrael-beytenu-knesset-slates-for-upcoming-election/; Wikipedia bios). Slogan: "We are the Likud of yesteryear" (class doc 11c).
- Among Jewish first-time voters aged 18–22, YB polls 6% (Midgam/N12, Sept 15–22, n=301, https://www.jpost.com/israel-election-2026/article-909394). No breakdown by origin is given.
- People of Israel (Ofer Winter) is estimated to be drawing "roughly another seat from Yisrael Beytenu." These are party sources, not a poll (JPost 2026-10-04, https://www.jpost.com/israel-election-2026/article-910503).

## 3. Attitudes on the six issues

**Main finding: no recent primary cross-tab on these issues for FSU immigrants was found.** IDI's Democracy Index 2025 does not break results down by FSU origin. IDI's 2023 religion-state statistical report has no FSU breakdown (https://en.idi.org.il/articles/49002). Hiddush's 2025 Religion and State Index shows only partial results, with no FSU breakdown (https://hiddush.org/article-23687-0-2025_Religion_and_State_Index.aspx). Pew 2016 asked its questions in Russian but publishes almost nothing broken out for this group. What exists:

| Issue | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Haredi draft | 94% support a mandatory draft law for all citizens, "with almost no difference between political camps" | Lazar 2025 |
| Religion-state | 37% support "religion-state separation" (wording not given) | Lazar 2025 |
| Religion-state | Belief in God: 55% of first-generation FSU immigrants vs 70% of the second generation | Pew, fieldwork 2014–15 |
| Economy | Cost of living is the top concern for 68%; 74% dissatisfied with monetary policy; 72% dissatisfied with the country's direction | Lazar 2025 |
| War/security | 56% concerned about personal and national security | Lazar 2025 |
| Netanyahu / courts (proxy) | 69% do not want Netanyahu to continue as PM | Lazar 2025 |
| West Bank | No group data. Khanin says the community remains in the "right-wing political camp" and backs Israel's Jewish identity "in its ethno-national sense" | Khanin 2023 |
| Ukraine war | IDI's March 2022 survey (501 FSU-immigrant Jews) found "no substantial difference" from other Jews on taking in refugees | https://en.idi.org.il/articles/38624 |

The 37% figure for religion-state separation looks low for a group with hundreds of thousands of people who cannot marry in Israel. "Separation" may be read as something broader than civil marriage. **Don't use it without the question wording.** There is no survey number for this group on civil marriage specifically, or on who counts as a Jew. The party positions are clear: YB's five-point religion-state pledge puts civil marriage and divorce first (class doc 11c).

## 4. Lived texture

- **The Liberman phenomenon.** He founded YB in 1999 as a party for Soviet immigrants. Its peak was 15 seats in 2009. His April 2019 refusal to join Netanyahu over the draft law set off five elections in a row. Since then he has tried to move the party from an immigrant party to a secular-nationalist party ("Likud of yesteryear"), and the locality data in section 2 fit that shift. Hanin (France24, 2020): "The core of Lieberman voters will stay faithful to him, despite Netanyahu's efforts" (https://www.france24.com/en/20200301-israel-s-netanyahu-tries-to-poach-russian-vote-from-old-rival).
- **The 1.5 and second generation.** People who came as children (the "1.5 generation") moved from being "guests" to people trying to shape the country (Lily Galili, Brookings, 2020-09-21, https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-other-tribe-israels-russian-speaking-community-and-how-it-is-changing-the-country/). The second generation is more religious than its parents (Pew). According to Riddle (2019, https://ridl.io/israel-s-russian-speaking-minority-political-force-in-the-knesset/), later generations "are more likely to vote for centrist parties."
- **The post-2022 arrivals.** Most are not Jewish under halacha. Many are educated, secular urban professionals leaving Russia because of the war. A majority of the 2014–2025 cohort is considering leaving Israel (IDI 2025). Their politics are contested in the data: Lazar's subsample leans toward a Likud figure, while the Elkayam-Shalem study shows opposition and left-leaning views. In 2022, MK Shlomo Karhi (Likud) said the grandchild clause had brought "'comfort' migrants here under the guise of Judaism." MK Yoel Razvozov replied that such reports "besmirched a population of a million people, including soldiers, police officers, teachers, engineers, doctors and others" (ToI 2022-11-17).
- **Voices.**
  - Lena Russovsky, journalist and activist of the "1.5 generation": "Israeli politicians have finally realized they have to address one million voters who have been here for 30 years" (ToI 2019-07-26).
  - Alex Grinberg, commentator born in Russia: "Russian voters' main political agenda is integration into Israeli society" (France24 2020).
  - Vladimir Beliak (MK, Yesh Atid), 2022: "Judaism doesn't belong to you. It belongs to all of us. You won't revoke the 'Law of Return,' you won't stop immigration, and you won't turn hundreds of thousands of Israelis into third-class citizens" (ToI 2022-11-17).
  - All three were seen only through WebFetch summaries. Check the exact wording against the original pages before publishing.
- None of these voices is an ordinary community member, and none speaks after Oct 7. A voice from the 2022–26 period is still needed.

## Source disagreements
1. **Size:** ~1.3M speakers / 15% (Wikipedia) vs ~1.25M immigrants since 1989 / 13% of the population (Khanin) vs ~1M voters / 15% (Lazar) vs 770k voters / 12% (Hanin 2019) vs 5.3% of Jews by self-ID (IDI 2025). These count different populations.
2. **Not recognized as Jewish:** 300k+ (Khanin, 1989–2019 arrivals only) vs ~400k (ToI 2019) vs ~500k "no religion" (Knesset RIC 2022, all origins).
3. **Non-Jewish share in 1990:** 6.8% (Knesset RIC) vs 4% (Reinharz/DellaPergola via Wikipedia). The 1990s figures vary across sources: 12–20% (ToI 2019, Khanin) vs 4–7%.
4. **Post-2022 arrivals' politics:** Lazar (Likud's Edelstein 37%, Liberman 32%) vs Elkayam-Shalem (45% opposition, 13% coalition, 27% self-ID left).
5. **Quote attribution:** "Immigrants from the former Soviet Union believe in strong leadership more than they believe in the democratic apparatus." ToI (2019) attributes this to Zeev Hanin; *Eurasian Times* (2026) attributes it to Lilia Remennick. Don't use it until this is resolved.
6. **Post-2022 arrivals count:** 125,000 (*Eurasian Times*) vs ~136,000 FSU arrivals 2022–25 (CBS via JVL, class doc) vs ~100,000 from Russia, Ukraine and Belarus (JPost 2025-09-20, class doc).

## Framing choices for Daniel
- **Group name:** "Russian-speaking Israelis" or "immigrants from the former Soviet Union and their children." Many come from Ukraine, which makes "Russian" a sensitive label after 2022. Israelis say "Rusim" colloquially.
- **Status term:** "not recognized as Jewish by the Rabbinate" or "not Jewish under halacha" or the CBS category "no religion / others."
- **Grandchild-clause arrivals:** use the label "war aliyah" / "Putin aliyah," or describe them only by arrival years. Karhi's term "comfort migrants" should only appear inside a quote.
- **Liberman's territorial plan:** "population exchange / land swap" (his framing) or "transfer of Arab towns" (critics' framing).
- **Lazar poll:** use it with a disclosure that the lead researcher was a YB MK, or leave it out.
- **Nof HaGalil:** mention the municipality's refusal to approve mosques, churches and Arabic schools, or leave it out.
- **Spelling:** Liberman or Lieberman.
