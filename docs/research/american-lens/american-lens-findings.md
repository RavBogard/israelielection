## 1. Why "pro-Israel" is not an Israeli category

**What the American groups say about themselves.**
- AIPAC: "AIPAC is an American grassroots organization that works to strengthen the U.S.-Israel partnership." Mission: "to encourage and persuade the U.S. government to enact specific policies…" On party: "AIPAC's members are Democrats and Republicans. We support pro-Israel candidates on both sides of the aisle and oppose anti-Israel candidates on both sides." (AIPAC, About, https://www.aipac.org/about). The page says nothing about Israeli parties or Israeli elections.
- J Street: "Pro-Israel, pro-peace, pro-democracy Americans to promote US policies that embody our deeply held Jewish and democratic values." (J Street, About, https://jstreet.org/about-us/)
- Jewish Voice for Peace (board statement, 2018–19): "We unequivocally oppose Zionism"; describes Zionism as "a settler-colonial movement, establishing an apartheid state where Jews have more rights than others." (JVP, https://www.jewishvoiceforpeace.org/resource/zionism/)

**What is worth noticing.** All three define themselves by what they want the *U.S. government* to do. Their target is Congress and the White House, not the Knesset. "Pro-Israel" in this sense is a position in American politics about American policy.

Inside Israel the word does no work. Every Jewish list on the 2026 ballot is Zionist, from Otzma Yehudit to the Democrats; the fights are among Zionists about the draft, the courts, the war, the West Bank and religion (class docs 11a–11d). The Arab lists are not Zionist and do not claim to be, and they are part of the Israeli electorate (section 5). There is no Israeli party whose platform is "being pro-Israel," and no Israeli counterpart to an American lobby that rates candidates on Israel. The nearest Israeli analogues — Peace Now on the left, Im Tirtzu or Regavim on the right — are Israeli civil-society groups pushing Israeli policy, not lobbies for a foreign government's policy. (No source opened for the analogues; this is a framing claim for Daniel.)

**How American Jews see these groups (2026).** Mellman Group for the Jewish Electorate Institute, 800 registered Jewish voters, fieldwork March 13–23 (2026): AIPAC 39% favorable / 29% unfavorable / 32% unsure; among Jewish Democrats 29 / 37 / 33. DMFI 32 / 16. J Street 18 / 19, with 46% never having heard of it. 39% said it is "more important than ever" for pro-Israel groups to lead advocacy; 37% feared it could backfire. Jewish Republicans 67% for strong pro-Israel advocacy, Democrats 28%. (Jewish Insider, April 2026, https://jewishinsider.com/2026/04/survey-jewish-voters-pro-israel-engagement-aipac-dmfi/)

**Who is "pro-Israel" in America.** In Pew's March 2026 survey of U.S. adults (n=3,507, fieldwork March 23–29, 2026), "Jewish Americans and White evangelical Protestants have mostly positive views of Israel, at 64% and 65%, respectively." "56% of Jewish Americans have little or no confidence in Netanyahu on world affairs." Overall, 37% of Americans view Israel favorably (60% unfavorable), down from 55/42 in 2022; 80% of Democrats are unfavorable; Republicans 58% favorable. (Pew, 2026-04-07, https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/07/negative-views-of-israel-netanyahu-continue-to-rise-among-americans-especially-young-people/)

Worth noticing: American Jews are no more favorable to Israel than white evangelicals, and the American "pro-Israel" coalition is now largely a partisan Republican one. That is an American realignment. It has no Israeli counterpart.

## 2. Why Israeli "left" and "right" don't line up with American ones

**The main axis is the conflict, not the economy.** The Chapel Hill Expert Survey for Israel (Zur and Bakker, *Party Politics*, 2025; 2021 and 2022 elections) found Israeli party competition runs on four dimensions: a general left-right dimension (40% of variance in 2021) "overwhelmed by parties' positions on policies related to the Arab-Israeli conflict"; a socio-cultural dimension (24%, rising to 31% in 2022); an economic dimension (19%); and populism (13%). Correlation of general left-right with economic left-right is 0.65–0.77 in Israel versus 0.86–0.94 in Belgium and the Netherlands. Shas and UTJ take "right-wing positions on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict and left-wing economic positions." (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13540688231218917; abstract-level summary, full text not read)

**Group identity predicts the vote better than income.** Shamir and Arian (APSR 1999), on Israeli elections 1969–1996: "The capacity of social cleavages to structure the vote has been maintained," driven by "issues involving identity dilemmas" that "amalgamate policy issues and social allegiances." (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/collective-identity-and-electoral-competition-in-israel/7C4B80ED5E6346291A2BB3FDBEC53485). Zeedan (*Societies*, 2024; 2020 locality results with CBS data): "voting behavior is better explained by affiliation to the social group… than by other socioeconomic characteristics such as education level, economic status, or geographic location." (https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/14/9/177)

**The income pattern runs against the American stereotype.** IDI pooled six polls of 3,948 voters from the March 2021 election: 46% of Yesh Atid voters reported above-average income; 46% of Likud voters reported below-average income (29% above). Likud voters: 58% Sephardi/Mizrahi, 21% Ashkenazi; Yesh Atid 50% Ashkenazi. (IDI, 2022-08-01, https://en.idi.org.il/articles/39409). The party of the "right" draws the poorer, more traditional voter; the "center-left" draws the richer, more secular one. (Americans will note a partial rhyme with the U.S. education realignment. Flag for Daniel whether to say so.)

**How Israeli Jews place themselves.**
- IDI, Jewish respondents, 1–7 scale (1–3 left, 4 center, 5–7 right): right 39% (1986), 29% (1995), 46% (2019), 62% (2022); center 25 / 28 / 33 / 24; left 23 / 36 / 17 / 11. (Times of Israel on IDI data, https://www.timesofisrael.com/israeli-jewish-voters-moved-significantly-rightward-in-recent-years-data-shows/)
- JPPI, July 2026, Jewish respondents: right 66% (63% July 2025), center 13% (10%), left 19% (22%). (Times of Israel, https://www.timesofisrael.com/battle-for-the-center-large-majority-of-israelis-already-know-how-theyll-vote-poll/). JPPI's scale gives a far smaller "center" than IDI's; the two series should not be joined on one line.
- Pew 2014–15 (n=5,601): Haredim 52% center, 47% right; Datiim 56% right; Hilonim 62% center, 14% left. (https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/israels-religiously-divided-society/; older than two years)

**What "left" and "right" predict.** Pew spring 2025, "a way can be found for Israel and an independent Palestinian state to coexist peacefully": left 54%, center 29%, right 7%. (https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/06/03/views-of-the-potential-for-lasting-peace/). The label tracks the Palestinian question that sharply.

**The religious parties are economically left.** CHES (above). The Haredi parties' core demands — yeshiva stipends, draft exemption, child allowances, funding for their school systems — are demands on the state budget (class doc 11a). Pew 2014–15 found nearly half of Haredim placed themselves in the center, not on the right.

**The "center" is security-hawkish and socially liberal.** IDI, Aug 31–Sept 3, 2026 (605 Jews, 150 Arabs): among centrist Jews the top vote consideration is economy and cost of living (40.5%), security fourth (25%). (https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177). Only 29% of centrists think coexistence with a Palestinian state is possible (Pew 2025). The leading centrist lists are led by former chiefs of staff or commando officers (Eisenkot, Bennett) and run on universal draft and the courts, not on a Palestinian state (class docs 11c). In American terms this bloc is hawkish on security and liberal on religion and the courts — a combination neither U.S. party offers.

## 3. Where the fights rhyme and where they don't

**Judiciary.**
- Israel has no written constitution; Basic Laws serve as the supreme framework, and the Supreme Court has for decades claimed power to strike down legislation. The Knesset passed a law ending "reasonableness" review on July 24, 2023; the Court struck it down on January 1, 2024, 8–7. On March 27, 2025 the Knesset passed a law changing the Judicial Selection Committee. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Israeli_judicial_reform)
- The committee has nine members (justice minister, a second minister, two MKs, two Bar Association members, the Chief Justice and two justices); a Supreme Court appointment needs 7 of 9. The 2025 law removes the Bar members and takes effect only when the next Knesset is seated. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_Selection_Committee_(Israel)). Wikipedia's description of who replaces the Bar members is unclear; check against the law text or the courts research file before publishing.
- **Rhyme:** both countries fight over whether unelected judges check elected majorities, and over who picks judges. **Difference:** in the U.S. the president nominates and the Senate confirms, inside a written constitution with life tenure; in Israel a mixed committee picks judges and there is no constitution to stand on. The American right wants judges to defer to the text; the Israeli right wants judges to defer to the Knesset. The 2025 law going live with the 26th Knesset puts the courts on this ballot in a way no U.S. election does.
- Trust in the Supreme Court splits by religiosity: Haredim 3%, secular 66% (IDI Democracy Index 2025; class doc, not reopened).

**Religion and state.**
- No civil marriage: "Marriage can be performed only under the auspices of the religious community to which couples belong, and inter-faith marriages performed within the country are not legally recognized." The Chief Rabbinate and rabbinical courts govern Jewish marriage. Couples marry abroad (Cyprus) or by videoconference with Utah officiants; the Lod court's recognition was upheld by the Supreme Court on March 7, 2023. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_in_Israel)
- Pew 2014–15: Reform 3% and Conservative 2% of Israeli Jews, versus 35% and 18% of U.S. Jews. (https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/comparisons-between-jews-in-israel-and-the-u-s/). Halakha as state law for Jews: Haredim 86% and Datiim 69% in favor; Hilonim 90% opposed. Public transport on Shabbat should stop: Hilonim 6%. (https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/israels-religiously-divided-society/)
- **Rhyme:** a religious-conservative bloc versus secular liberals over public life. **Difference:** Israel has an established religious authority; the fight is over how much of it to keep, not whether the state may endorse religion. The Israeli secular camp is mostly not Reform or Conservative; it is secular. The Reform movement's best-known Israeli political figure, Rabbi Gilad Kariv, runs third on the Democrats list (class doc 11c, not reopened).

**Immigration.**
- The Law of Return (1950) gives every Jew the right to immigrate; the 1970 amendment extended it to "a child and a grandchild of a Jew, the spouse of a Jew, the spouse of a child of a Jew and the spouse of a grandchild of a Jew." Converts (Orthodox, Reform or Conservative) qualify. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Return). The coalition revived an effort to limit the grandchild clause in May 2025 (ToI headline cited on that Wikipedia page; ToI article not opened).
- 98% of Israeli Jews said Diaspora Jews have the right to immigrate under the Law of Return (Pew 2014–15, https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/views-of-the-jewish-state-and-the-diaspora/).
- 71.7% of 2020 FSU immigrants were not Jewish under halakha (Knesset Research Center via ToI 2022; class doc, not reopened).
- **Rhyme:** "who belongs" fights. **Difference:** Israeli immigration law is built on descent and Jewish status, and the live dispute is about the grandchild clause and conversion, not border enforcement. An American "pro-immigration / anti-immigration" frame does not sort Israeli parties.

**Guns.**
- No constitutional right to bear arms; civilians need a license from the Ministry of National Security; eligibility was narrow before 2018. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gun_politics_in_Israel)
- After Oct 7, Ben-Gvir loosened rules by emergency directive. He said "more than 260,000 people have applied for a gun license since October 7" (ToI, 2023-12-04); his office claimed "230,000 new firearm licenses" (JPost, 2025-09-01); Otzma's 2026 campaign claims ~400,000 (C14; class doc 11b). All three are the minister's own claims, found second-hand via Wikipedia and class docs; not verified.
- 2005 baseline: 237,000 private citizens and 154,000 security guards licensed (Wikipedia, same page).
- **Rhyme:** the right expands civilian gun access. **Difference:** in Israel it is a security argument run by a minister with a licensing system, not a rights claim; there is no Israeli NRA equivalent and no Second Amendment.

## 4. The data on the gap

**Two states, the same question, two publics** ("a way can be found for Israel and an independent Palestinian state to coexist peacefully"):
- U.S. Jews: 61% (2013); 63% (Pew, fieldwork Nov 2019–June 2020, n=4,718). Jewish Democrats ~70%; Orthodox two-thirds say not possible; Jewish Republicans a slim majority not possible. (https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/05/11/u-s-jews-connections-with-and-attitudes-toward-israel/; 2013 figure from https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/comparisons-between-jews-in-israel-and-the-u-s/)
- Israeli Jews: 43% (2014–15); 16% (spring 2025); 16% (spring 2026). All Israelis: 50% (2013), 35% (2023), 26% (2024), 21% (2025), 17% (2026); 56% now say it is not possible, a first. 2026 fieldwork April 5–May 6, n=1,001. (https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/09/pg_2026.09.15_coexistence_report.pdf; https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/06/03/views-of-the-potential-for-lasting-peace/)
- Worth noticing: the gap opened mostly because Israeli opinion moved, not American. No post-2020 Pew figure for U.S. Jews on this question was found.

**Settlements.** Pew 2013/2014–15: settlements help Israel's security — Israeli Jews 42%, U.S. Jews 17% (44% of U.S. Jews say they hurt). By Israeli group: Datiim 68%, Haredim 50%, Masortim 45%, Hilonim 31%. (Pew 2016 pages above). Over ten years old; no newer paired measure found.

**Netanyahu.**
- U.S. Jews 2020: 40% excellent/good (13 / 27); Orthodox 77% positive; Jewish Republicans 80% positive; Jewish Democrats 70% negative. (Pew 2021, above)
- U.S. Jews Sept 2025: 68% negative on his leadership, 48% "poor" (Washington Post/SSRS, n=815, Sept 2–9, 2025, MOE ±4.7; via JTA, https://www.jta.org/2025/10/06/united-states/poll-40-of-american-jews-believe-israel-is-committing-genocide-in-gaza)
- U.S. Jews March 2026: 56% little or no confidence (Pew, above).
- Israelis 2025: 45% favorable, 53% unfavorable (Pew, n=998, Feb 5–Mar 11, 2025, https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/06/03/israeli-public-is-increasingly-skeptical-about-lasting-peace/). No Jewish-only breakdown on that page.
- Four different question wordings. They can be shown side by side but not as one series.

**Religion.** See section 3: Reform 3% / Conservative 2% in Israel; intermarriage 44% of married U.S. Jews vs 2% of Israeli Jews (Pew 2016 comparisons page).

**American Jewish attitudes after Oct 7.**
- Washington Post/SSRS, Sept 2025: 61% say Israel committed war crimes; 39% (JTA text; headline rounds to 40%) say genocide; 50% of 18–34s say genocide. 46% approve and 48% oppose Israel's military action; Jewish Republicans 85% approve, Democrats 31%. Emotional attachment: over half overall; 36% of 18–34s, 68% of 65+. 75% say Israel's existence is vital to the Jewish future. (JTA, above). Exact question wording not seen.
- AJC/SSRS, Sept 26–Oct 29, 2025, n=1,222: 77% say caring about Israel is important to their Jewish identity (43% very); 83% say "Israel has no right to exist" is antisemitic; 78% feel less safe since Oct 7. (https://www.ajc.org/AntisemitismReport2025/AmericanJews)
- Pew 2020 baseline: 58% very/somewhat attached (Orthodox 82%, Reform 58%; 18–29 48%, 65+ 67%); 45% say caring about Israel is "essential." (https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/05/21/u-s-jews-have-widely-differing-views-on-israel/)
- JEI spring 2026 poll exists with Iran-war and Zionism items; the landing page carries no numbers and the PDFs were not opened.

**Paired U.S.–Israeli Jewish surveys (JPPI).**
- Aug 14–18, 2025, U.S. n=639 "connected" Jews: famine reports true — U.S. Jews 48%, Israeli Jews 36%; worried Israel is becoming a pariah — 75% vs 62%; Israel "is winning or has won" — 32% vs 48%. 47% of U.S. Jews say Israel should weigh Diaspora consequences; 45% say victory regardless. (https://jppi.org.il/en/jppi-survey-american-jewish-views-on-the-war-in-gaza/)
- Sept 2026 Jewish People Voice Index: Israeli government policy contributes to antisemitism "to a large extent" — U.S. Jews 39% (31% in Sept 2025), Israeli Jews 28%; no connection — 23% (32% in 2025) vs 44%. Israel should not change policy at all — 37% vs 57%. Optimistic about Israel's future — 19% vs 43%; strongly liberal U.S. Jews 4%, strongly conservative 51%. (JPost, 2026-09-24, https://www.jpost.com/diaspora/antisemitism/article-909578)
- JPPI's U.S. panel is a "connected" sample, more engaged than all U.S. Jews. Say so on the page.

**How Israelis see American and Diaspora Jews.**
- Pew 2014–15: 75% of Israeli Jews share a common destiny with U.S. Jews; 59% say American Jews have a good influence on how things go in Israel, 6% bad; 69% say a thriving Diaspora is vital to Jewish survival. (https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/views-of-the-jewish-state-and-the-diaspora/). Over ten years old.
- IDI, Dec 28, 2025–Jan 1, 2026 (602 Jews, 150 Arabs): about three-quarters of Jews say Israel is safer for Jews than abroad (left 41%, center 69%, right 83%). Support for government help to Diaspora communities: pressing foreign governments on security 90%, sending emissaries 80%, direct involvement in security ~60%, financial support 50%. (https://en.idi.org.il/articles/62950)
- "Jewish first" identity among Israeli Jews 57%, up from 34% a year earlier (Diaspora Affairs Ministry survey, Feb 2026, via ToI; class doc 05, not reopened).
- Worth noticing: recent Israeli polling treats Diaspora Jews mostly as people to protect or bring home, not as a constituency with a voice in Israeli policy. No recent Israeli survey found that asks whether Diaspora Jews should influence Israeli policy.

## 5. The misreadings

Each is a sentence an American reader is likely to bring; the evidence is above.

1. **"Likud is the Republican Party."** Likud's voters are poorer and more Mizrahi-traditional than the center-left's (IDI 2021 voter data); Likud has no written 2026 platform and no stated economic program this cycle (class doc 11a, Ynet 2026-09-30). Israel's right-left axis is the conflict, not taxes (CHES).
2. **"Left and right mean the same thing there."** The general left-right dimension is dominated by the Arab-Israeli conflict; economic positions are a separate, weaker dimension (CHES). A "right-wing" Israeli can back a large welfare state.
3. **"The ultra-Orthodox are the religious right."** Haredi parties are hawkish on the conflict but economically left (CHES); in 2014–15 more Haredim called themselves center (52%) than right (47%) (Pew). Their core issue is the draft exemption and yeshiva funding, not territory.
4. **"Israeli Arabs can't vote."** Palestinian citizens vote and hold seats. 2022: Ra'am 5 seats, Hadash–Ta'al 5; Balad fell below the 3.25% threshold with 2.91% (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Israeli_legislative_election). Arab turnout 53.2% (IDI; class doc). Ra'am sat in the Bennett-Lapid coalition from June 13, 2021, "the first to include an independent Arab Israeli party as an official member of the governing coalition" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-sixth_government_of_Israel).
5. **"The election is about Gaza and a Palestinian state."** Jewish voters' top considerations: security 39%, economy and cost of living 38%, conscription 27%, religion and state 26% (IDI, Sept 2026). Arab voters: crime in Arab society 53.5%, economy 46%. A Palestinian state is not on the ballot: 16% of Israeli Jews think coexistence is possible (Pew 2026).
6. **"Criticizing Netanyahu is being anti-Israel."** Every Jewish opposition list is Zionist and many are led by former generals; 53% of Israelis viewed Netanyahu unfavorably in 2025 (Pew).
7. **"Reform and Conservative Judaism are Israel's liberal religious mainstream."** 3% and 2% of Israeli Jews (Pew 2014–15). The Israeli liberal camp is secular; the fight is over the Rabbinate's monopoly on marriage, not denominational pluralism.
8. **"Israel's Supreme Court works like ours."** No written constitution; judges picked by a nine-member committee needing 7 votes; the 2025 change takes effect with the next Knesset — so the courts are on this ballot.
9. **"Israelis vote for a prime minister."** They vote for a party list; the PM is whoever can form a coalition of 61 (class docs 02, 07). (Direct election of the PM existed 1996–2001; not reopened here.)
10. **"Settlers are religious zealots."** Settlers are roughly 36% Haredi, 36% national-religious and 28% secular (Yesha data via ToI 2023; class doc, not reopened). The two largest settlements, Modiin Illit and Beitar Illit, are Haredi towns (class doc 05).
11. **"American Jews are Israel's base in America."** Jewish Americans (64% favorable) are about where white evangelicals are (65%), and Israel's support now runs mostly through Republicans (Pew 2026).
12. **"The Law of Return is like U.S. immigration policy."** It is a descent-based right covering grandchildren and spouses; the live fight is the grandchild clause and conversion (Law of Return; class doc 05 on non-halakhic immigrants).

## Source disagreements

- Washington Post genocide figure: JTA text says 39%; JTA/Forward/Ynet headlines say 40%; SSRS says "about 4 in 10." Use 39% with "about 4 in 10."
- Israeli Jewish self-placement: IDI (2022) right 62 / center 24 / left 11; JPPI (July 2026) right 66 / center 13 / left 19. Different scales and question wording; the center gap is mostly a measurement difference.
- Pew 2025 coexistence: the 2025 page gives Israeli Arabs 40% possible; the 2026 report gives 23% possible with 46% not answering. Real change or a refusal effect — Pew's own note points to refusals.
- Gun licenses: 260,000 applications (Dec 2023), 230,000 new licenses (Sept 2025), ~400,000 (Otzma campaign 2026). All from the minister's side; no independent count found.
- Netanyahu measures for U.S. Jews (job rating 2020, leadership rating 2025, confidence 2026) and for Israelis (favorability 2025) are four different questions.
- The 2025 Judicial Selection Committee law: Wikipedia's account of the replacement members is internally unclear. Check against the courts research file.
- AJC 2025 media-bias item came back from the fetch as 43% "biased for Hamas" / 33% "biased for Israel"; I did not trust the extraction and left it out.
