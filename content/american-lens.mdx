American readers usually come to Israeli politics with a map drawn for American politics: pro-Israel and critical of Israel, left and right, religious and secular. Each of those labels means something in Israel, but rarely the same thing. This page sets out where the American map fits and where it misleads.

## Why "pro-Israel" is not an Israeli category

The best-known American organizations on Israel each define themselves in American terms: by the U.S. policy they want, or by the American Jews they organize.

- **AIPAC:** "AIPAC is an American grassroots organization that works to strengthen the U.S.-Israel partnership." Its mission is "to encourage and persuade the U.S. government to enact specific policies that create a strong, enduring and mutually beneficial relationship with our ally Israel" ([AIPAC, About](https://www.aipac.org/about)).
- **J Street:** "J Street organizes pro-Israel, pro-peace, pro-democracy Americans to promote US policies" ([J Street, About](https://jstreet.org/about-us/)).
- **Jewish Voice for Peace:** "The Jewish Voice for Peace family of organizations organizes a grassroots, multiracial, cross-class, intergenerational movement of U.S. Jews into solidarity with the Palestinian freedom struggle" ([JVP, About](https://www.jewishvoiceforpeace.org/about/)). On Zionism: "We unequivocally oppose Zionism because it is counter to those ideals," which it names as "justice, equality and freedom for all people" ([JVP](https://www.jewishvoiceforpeace.org/resource/zionism/)).

All three organize Americans and work on American policy and politics, not on Knesset elections. "Pro-Israel," in the sense these groups argue over, is a position in American politics about American policy. Israeli voters are not choosing between pro-Israel and anti-Israel parties. When Jewish Israelis name what will decide their vote, they name foreign affairs and security, the economy and cost of living, conscription, and religion and state ([IDI, September 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)).

American views of Israel also divide along American lines of religion and party. In March 2026, 64% of Jewish Americans and 65% of White evangelical Protestants viewed Israel favorably, against 37% of all Americans, down from 55% in 2022. Republicans were 58% favorable; 80% of Democrats were unfavorable ([Pew Research Center, March 23–29, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/07/negative-views-of-israel-netanyahu-continue-to-rise-among-americans-especially-young-people/)). Among registered Jewish voters, 39% viewed AIPAC favorably and 29% unfavorably; 46% had never heard of J Street ([Mellman Group poll for the Jewish Electorate Institute, March 2026, via Jewish Insider](https://jewishinsider.com/2026/04/survey-jewish-voters-pro-israel-engagement-aipac-dmfi/)).

## Why Israeli left and right don't line up with American ones

**The main axis is the conflict, not the economy.** An expert survey of Israeli party positions in the 2021 and 2022 elections found that the general left-right dimension is "overwhelmed by parties' positions on policies related to the Arab-Israeli conflict" ([Zur and Bakker, *Party Politics*, CHES-Israel 2021–22](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13540688231218917)). Economic positions are a separate, weaker dimension. The same study places Shas and United Torah Judaism, the two Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) parties, on the right on the conflict and on the left on economics.

**Group identity predicts the vote better than income.** A study of Israel's 2020 locality results found that "voting behavior is better explained by affiliation to the social group… than by other socioeconomic characteristics such as education level, economic status, or geographic location" ([Zeedan, *Societies*, 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/14/9/177)). The income pattern runs against what an American might expect. In the March 2021 election, 46% of Likud voters reported below-average household income and 29% above-average; 46% of Yesh Atid voters reported above-average income. Likud's voters were 58% Sephardi or Mizrahi, Yesh Atid's 50% Ashkenazi ([IDI, six pooled polls, n=3,948, 2021](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/39409)).

**"Left" and "right" track the Palestinian question.** In spring 2025, 54% of Israelis on the left and 7% on the right thought Israel and an independent Palestinian state could coexist peacefully ([Pew, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/06/03/views-of-the-potential-for-lasting-peace/)). Israeli Jews have also moved right on their own placement: 62% put themselves on the right in 2022, up from 46% in 2019.

**The religious are not simply "the right."** In Pew's 2014–15 Israel survey, the most recent found with this breakdown, 52% of Haredim placed themselves in the center and 47% on the right; among secular Jews (hilonim), 62% chose the center and 14% the left ([Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/israels-religiously-divided-society/)). The "center" in 2026 is defined by other issues: centrist Jews name the economy and cost of living as their top consideration (40.5%), with security fourth (25%) ([IDI, September 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)).

## Where the fights rhyme and where they don't

**Courts.** Both countries argue over how far courts should check elected majorities, and over who picks the judges. The differences are large. Israel has no written constitution; its Basic Laws serve instead. On January 1, 2024, a full 15-justice panel struck down the 2023 law ending "reasonableness" review, 8–7 ([NPR, January 1, 2024](https://www.npr.org/2024/01/01/1222400537/israel-supreme-court-ruling-judiciary-changes)). Judges are chosen by a nine-member committee of ministers, lawmakers, lawyers and justices, not nominated by an executive and confirmed by a legislature. A 2025 law changes that committee and takes effect when the next Knesset convenes, after the October 27 vote ([Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/regime-change-high-court-tears-into-coalition-bid-to-control-judicial-appointments/)). The courts are on this ballot. See [courts and the judicial overhaul](/issues/courts).

**Religion and state.** "Israel does not allow civil marriage, and Jewish marriages conducted in Israel must be sanctioned by Orthodox rabbis" ([Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/israels-religiously-divided-society/)). The Israeli argument is over how much established religious authority to keep, not whether the state may endorse religion at all. And the Israeli liberal camp is mostly secular, not Reform or Conservative: in Pew's surveys, 3% of Israeli Jews identified as Reform and 2% as Conservative, against 35% and 18% of U.S. Jews ([Pew, 2016; Israel data 2014–15, U.S. data 2013](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/comparisons-between-jews-in-israel-and-the-u-s/)). See [religion and state](/issues/religion-state).

**Immigration.** Both countries argue over who belongs. Israel's argument is built on descent and Jewish status. In 2014–15, 98% of Israeli Jews agreed that "it is the birthright of all Jews around the world to make aliyah to Israel" ([Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/views-of-the-jewish-state-and-the-diaspora/)). The live dispute is about the law's grandchild clause and who counts as Jewish: by a Knesset Research and Information Center count, 71.7% of 2020's immigrants from the former Soviet Union were not Jewish according to most interpretations of Jewish law ([Times of Israel, November 17, 2022](https://www.timesofisrael.com/data-shows-major-drop-in-immigrants-from-former-soviet-union-considered-jewish/)). See [Russian-speaking Israelis](/communities/russian-speakers).

## The data on the gap

On the question Pew has asked both publics, the gap opened because Israelis moved. In 2013, 61% of U.S. Jews thought Israel and an independent Palestinian state could coexist peacefully; in 2019–20, 63% did. Israeli Jews went from 43% in 2014–15 to 16% in 2025 and again in 2026. Where the same questions go to both publics, American Jews are more worried and Israeli Jews more confident. The Jewish People Policy Institute's U.S. sample is a panel of "connected" Jews, more engaged than American Jews as a whole.

<Chart id="american-lens.paired" />

American Jewish attachment to Israel remains high but uneven. In 2025, 77% of American Jews said caring about Israel is important to their Jewish identity, 43% very important ([AJC survey by SSRS, September 26–October 29, 2025](https://www.ajc.org/AntisemitismReport2025/AmericanJews)). In Pew's 2019–20 survey, 58% were very or somewhat emotionally attached to Israel: 48% of those aged 18–29 and 67% of those 65 and over ([Pew, 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/05/21/u-s-jews-have-widely-differing-views-on-israel/)).

Recent Israeli polling treats Diaspora Jews mostly as people to protect. About three-quarters of Jewish Israelis say Israel is safer for Jews than abroad, and 90% support pressing foreign governments on Diaspora Jews' security ([IDI, December 28, 2025–January 1, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/62950)). No recent Israeli survey found asks whether Diaspora Jews should have a say in Israeli policy.

## Misreadings

**"Left and right mean the same thing there."** Israel's main left-right divide is the conflict with the Palestinians; economic views are a separate, weaker dimension ([CHES-Israel](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13540688231218917)). A voter on the Israeli right can favor a large welfare state.

**"Likud is the party of the well-off."** In 2021, 46% of Likud voters reported below-average income; 46% of Yesh Atid voters reported above-average income ([IDI](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/39409)).

**"The Haredim are the religious right."** Their parties are hawkish on the conflict but economically on the left ([CHES-Israel](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13540688231218917)). See [Haredim](/communities/haredim).

**"The election is about a Palestinian state."** Jewish voters rank security, the cost of living and conscription highest ([IDI, September 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)), and 16% of Israeli Jews think a way can be found for Israel and an independent Palestinian state to coexist peacefully ([Pew, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/09/pg_2026.09.15_coexistence_report.pdf)). See [what is not on the ballot](/issues/not-on-ballot).

**"Arab citizens don't really take part."** Palestinian citizens of Israel vote and win seats: in 2022, Ra'am won 5 and Hadash–Ta'al 5, while Balad fell short of the 3.25% threshold with 2.9% ([IDI, 2022 results](https://en.idi.org.il/israeli-elections-and-parties/elections/2022/)). See [Palestinian citizens of Israel](/communities/palestinian-citizens).

**"Settlers are one religious movement."** About a third of settlers live in Haredi towns, and the settlements vote as three different blocs. See [Settlers](/communities/settlers).

**"Reform and Conservative Judaism are Israel's liberal religious mainstream."** They are 3% and 2% of Israeli Jews ([Pew, 2014–15 data](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/comparisons-between-jews-in-israel-and-the-u-s/)). The Israeli fight is over the Rabbinate's control of marriage, not denominational pluralism.

**"American Jews are Israel's strongest supporters in America."** In March 2026, Jewish Americans (64% favorable) were about where White evangelical Protestants were (65%) ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/07/negative-views-of-israel-netanyahu-continue-to-rise-among-americans-especially-young-people/)).
