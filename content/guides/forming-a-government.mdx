## What a government needs

Israelis do not vote for a prime minister. They vote for a party list, and the 120 seats are shared out in proportion to the vote (see [how seats are counted](/how-it-works/seats)). The government is then built inside the Knesset. Under Basic Law: The Government, "the Government holds office by virtue of the confidence of the Knesset," and the prime minister must be a Knesset member ([Basic Law: The Government, Knesset translation, amended through Aug 2023](https://main.knesset.gov.il/EN/activity/Documents/BasicLawsPDF/BasicLawTheGovernment.pdf)).

No single party has ever won a majority of the seats, so every Israeli government has been a coalition, with between three and nine parties in the coalitions formed after elections ([IDI, Ofer Kenig, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63976)).

A new government takes office only when the Knesset votes to express confidence in it. The prime minister-designate presents the ministers, how their portfolios are divided and the government's policy guidelines, and "the Government shall be formed once the Knesset has expressed confidence in it" (art. 13). The law does not demand an absolute majority: a government can be approved if more members vote for it than against it. In practice, IDI notes, governments formed after elections "have almost always enjoyed the confidence of a majority of Knesset members (at least 61 MKs)" ([IDI, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63976)). That is why 61 is the number that coalition arithmetic is built around, including in the [Coalition Builder](/).

A coalition also has to keep that support. The Knesset dissolves automatically if it does not pass a state budget by the end of March, or within 145 days of a new government taking office ([IDI, Assaf Shapira, Jan 28, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63274)).

## The clock

The process starts when the Central Elections Committee publishes the official results. The president then consults the representatives of every list entering the Knesset and, within seven days of the official results, gives "the task of forming a government" to one Knesset member who agrees to take it on (art. 7). This is the step usually called getting "the mandate."

The law gives the president no rule for choosing. IDI's explainer says the president "typically" picks the leader of the largest party, "but this is by no means mandatory"; the deciding factor is who seems to have the best prospect of winning the Knesset's confidence ([IDI, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63982)).

Put end to end, the time limits in articles 7 to 10 come to at most 117 days after the official results are published (a total computed from the law's own limits). If they all run out, the Knesset is treated as having dissolved itself, and the election falls on the last Tuesday before 90 days have passed (art. 11). IDI's summary: the law allows three attempts, and "in almost all cases, the leader who was given the first chance to form a government succeeded in doing so" ([IDI, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63976)).

## How long it takes

From election day to a sworn-in government has taken between 20 and 100 days in Israel. In the past two decades no government has been sworn in less than 50 days after an election ([IDI, Kenig, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63982)). The record is from 1955, when 100 days passed. In the state's first two decades the process had no time limit ([IDI, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63976)).

## 2019–2022: five elections in under four years

The four-year cycle broke down in 2019, and the clock was tested at almost every step.

After the April 9, 2019 election, Benjamin Netanyahu failed to form a coalition in the 42 days allowed. The deal fell apart over the bill on drafting Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) yeshiva students: Avigdor Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu insisted the bill pass unchanged, and the Haredi parties refused. The Knesset then voted 74–45 to dissolve itself and set a new election for September 17 ([Times of Israel, May 30, 2019](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-votes-for-new-elections-on-september-17-after-pm-fails-to-form-coalition/)). It was the first Israeli election that failed to produce a government ([Times of Israel, Dec 12, 2019](https://www.timesofisrael.com/israel-calls-another-election-for-march-the-third-in-a-year/)).

After September, every stage of the law was used. Netanyahu tried first and gave up. On October 23, 2019, President Reuven Rivlin gave the task to Benny Gantz of Blue and White, who had 28 days ([Times of Israel, Oct 23, 2019](https://www.timesofisrael.com/charged-with-forming-coalition-gantz-asks-netanyahu-to-join-vows-to-heal-rifts/)). Gantz failed too. Over the next 21 days no Knesset member gathered the 61 signatures needed for a third attempt, so the Knesset dissolved at midnight on December 11 and set an election for March 2, 2020 ([Times of Israel, Dec 12, 2019](https://www.timesofisrael.com/israel-calls-another-election-for-march-the-third-in-a-year/)).

In March 2020 Rivlin gave the first mandate to Gantz, not Netanyahu ([Jerusalem Post, Mar 16, 2020](https://www.jpost.com/Breaking-News/Gantz-arrives-at-Presidents-House-to-receive-mandate-to-form-govt-621147)). The two men then formed a rotation government, which was sworn in on May 17, 2020 by 73 votes to 46 ([Times of Israel, May 17, 2020](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/new-government-officially-sworn-in-with-parliamentary-vote/)). It never passed a 2020 budget, and the Knesset dissolved in December 2020. This is the only time an election has been brought forward because a budget failed ([IDI, Jan 28, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63274)).

After March 2021 Rivlin again gave Netanyahu the first chance, saying:

<Quote who="Reuven Rivlin" role="President of Israel" source="Al Jazeera" url="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2021/4/6/israels-president-to-announce-candidate-to-form-new-government" date="April 6, 2021">No candidate has a realistic chance of forming a government that will have the confidence of the Knesset.</Quote>

The government that followed had eight parties: Yesh Atid, Yamina, Blue and White, Yisrael Beytenu, Labor, New Hope, Meretz and Ra'am ([IDI, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63976)). Naftali Bennett became prime minister after a 60–59 vote, with one Ra'am member abstaining ([Times of Israel, June 13, 2021](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/bennett-lapid-government-wins-knesset-majority-netanyahu-is-out-after-12-years/)). A year later the Knesset dissolved itself 92–0 and set the fifth election for November 1, 2022 ([Times of Israel, June 30, 2022](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/lawmakers-finalize-vote-to-dissolve-parliament-head-for-new-elections/)). After the November election, 64 members recommended Netanyahu ([Al Jazeera, Nov 11, 2022](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/11/11/netanyahu-mandated-to-form-new-israeli-government)), and his government won its confidence vote 63–54 on December 29, 2022 ([Times of Israel, Dec 29, 2022](https://www.timesofisrael.com/netanyahu-resumes-role-as-pm-as-israels-most-hardline-government-ever-takes-office/)).

## Misreadings

**"The party with the most seats wins."** Winning the most seats brings no right to govern. After the 2009 election, President Shimon Peres gave the mandate to Netanyahu, whose Likud had 27 seats, instead of Tzipi Livni, whose Kadima had 28 ([IDI, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63976)). What counts is who can win the Knesset's confidence, and that is a question of blocs. The [Coalition Builder](/) lets a reader try out the combinations.

**"Israelis elect the prime minister directly."** Israel did this only for a short period. A separate ballot for prime minister was first used in 1996, when Netanyahu beat Shimon Peres on May 29 ([IDI, June 17, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/64732)). In IDI's words, direct elections were "quickly shelved in 2001, just five years after they were first introduced" ([IDI, Apr 20, 2021](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/34340)). Since then the prime minister has been whichever Knesset member can put a government together.

**"Sixty-one is a legal requirement."** The confidence vote needs more votes for the government than against it, not 61. The Bennett government took office with 60 ([Times of Israel, June 13, 2021](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/bennett-lapid-government-wins-knesset-majority-netanyahu-is-out-after-12-years/)). Sixty-one is the number a government needs to be safe: it is the majority that can name a candidate under article 10, or replace a sitting government by voting confidence in another (art. 28) ([Basic Law: The Government](https://main.knesset.gov.il/EN/activity/Documents/BasicLawsPDF/BasicLawTheGovernment.pdf)).

**"A coalition deal settles who governs until the next election."** A coalition agreement sets out each partner's commitments and the posts it gets ([IDI, Apr 15, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63982)), but it lasts only as long as the Knesset majority behind it. On the day the 2020 unity government was sworn in, Netanyahu promised to hand the premiership to Gantz on November 17, 2021 ([Times of Israel, May 17, 2020](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog-may-17-2020/)). The handover never happened: the Knesset dissolved in December 2020 over the budget ([IDI, Jan 28, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63274)).

**"Election night settles it."** Election night only starts the clock. Of the five elections from 2019 to 2022, two produced no government at all. For the three that did, the swearing-in came 58 to 82 days after the vote (counted from the dates in the table above).
