## What is at stake

The conscription law makes every Israeli citizen who reaches 18 liable for service ([Zeidan Atashi, former Druze MK, via Jewish Virtual Library](https://jewishvirtuallibrary.org/druze-in-israel-and-the-question-of-compulsory-military-service)). In practice the army calls up Jewish men and women and Druze men ([Pew Research Center, March 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-tank/2016/03/21/5-facts-about-israeli-druze-a-unique-religious-and-ethnic-group/)), and Circassians; Muslim, Bedouin and Christian Arab citizens are not called up and may volunteer ([Atashi, via Jewish Virtual Library](https://jewishvirtuallibrary.org/druze-in-israel-and-the-question-of-compulsory-military-service)). Compulsory service "will remain at 32 months", the Knesset decided in July 2026, against the army's request for more ([Ynet, July 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/h1nremv4gl)). Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) men have, for most of the state's history, been able to defer service year after year while studying full-time in yeshiva. David Ben-Gurion, the first defense minister, ordered the deferral for students for whom "Torah is their calling" (*Torato umanuto*). It was capped at about 400 a year until 1970; a quota of 800 was set in 1975, and "following the coalition agreement of 1977, the quota was abolished altogether," the Supreme Court recounted in 1998 ([Supreme Court, HCJ 3267/97, Dec 9, 1998, via Cardozo Law School](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/rubinstein-v-minister-defense)). Why Palestinian citizens of Israel are not drafted is a separate question, covered on [Palestinian citizens of Israel](/communities/palestinian-citizens).

The Supreme Court struck down the laws that put the deferral into statute: the Tal Law in 2012, and a 2015 amendment in 2017 ([IDI, January 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63168)). On June 25, 2024, a unanimous nine-justice panel ruled there is "no legal basis for avoiding the recruitment of yeshiva students" and that the state may not fund the yeshivas of men who should be drafted ([Times of Israel, June 2024](https://www.timesofisrael.com/in-historic-ruling-high-court-says-government-must-begin-drafting-haredi-men-into-idf/)). In April 2026 it ordered personal sanctions, including loss of discounted housing and daycare subsidies ([IDI, April 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/64062)). On September 3, 2026, it voided a July 2026 law shielding yeshiva students from arrest. The ruling counted about 73,664 "draft evaders", the term used in Israeli debate (this page says yeshiva students who did not report), of whom 219 had been arrested ([Jerusalem Post, September 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-907553)).

Since October 7, 2023, the argument has been about manpower as much as principle. The IDF says it is short about 12,000 soldiers, 6,000–7,500 of them in combat roles ([IDI, January 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63168)). Average reserve duty was 94 days in 2024 and 78 in 2025 ([Times of Israel, June 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/average-reserve-duty-in-2025-was-78-days-37-of-reservists-are-parents-biggest-number-comes-from-tel-aviv/)).

Haredi enlistment has roughly doubled, from about 1,800 a year before the war to at least 4,000 in 2025–26, a preliminary figure, against an army target of 4,800 and about 14,000 Haredi men reaching draft age each year ([Times of Israel, July 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/idf-chief-tells-haredi-commanders-army-must-grow-as-data-shows-uptick-in-enlistment/); [Jerusalem Post, July 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-904035)). Draft orders are a different measure: in the 2024 draft year, about 24,000 initial orders produced 798 enlistees ([IDI, January 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63168)).

The two sides argue in different terms. Supporters of drafting Haredim speak of an equal burden; the court wrote that "in the midst of a severe war, the burden of inequality is more acute than ever" ([Times of Israel, June 2024](https://www.timesofisrael.com/in-historic-ruling-high-court-says-government-must-begin-drafting-haredi-men-into-idf/)). Haredi leaders speak of Torah study as "the spiritual defense of the People of Israel" and of the state's Jewish identity ([Times of Israel, April 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/haredi-leaders-assail-oppressive-courts-order-to-sanction-draft-evaders/)). In September 2026, rallies welcomed about 160 men released from military prison; gift bags handed to children read "The welcoming ceremony for prisoners of the Torah" ([Ynet, September 2026](https://ynetnews.com/article/bydixytqzl)).

The government's answer was the Bismuth bill, presented in November 2025. Its sponsors call it a draft law; critics call it an exemption bill. It set targets of 6,840 to 8,500 enlistees a year for four years, then "no less than 50 percent of the annual enlistment cohort", while letting full-time yeshiva students keep yearly deferrals to age 26 ([Times of Israel, November 2025](https://www.timesofisrael.com/bismuth-presents-bill-exempting-yeshiva-students-from-idf-draft-loosening-sanctions/)). IDI wrote that the October outline "does not establish an equal obligation for conscription" ([IDI, October 2025](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/61994)). The bill was shelved in March 2026, during the war with Iran ([VIN News, May 2026](https://vinnews.com/2026/05/17/haredi-parties-push-knesset-dissolution-as-draft-exemption-bill-returns-to-committee/)).

The draft split the coalition in its last year. Both factions of United Torah Judaism left the government over the stalled law in July 2025 ([Times of Israel, July 2025](https://www.timesofisrael.com/united-torah-judaism-quits-govt-to-protest-new-proposal-on-army-enlistment-exemptions/)), and the Knesset dissolved in July 2026, setting the vote for October 27. The Knesset was near the end of its four-year term anyway: the outgoing coalition "became the first Israeli government in decades to complete a full four-year term" ([JNS, July 2026](https://www.jns.org/news/israel-news/knesset-votes-to-dissolve-formally-sets-oct-27-election-date)). Money is part of the fight: after the 2024 ruling, NIS 480 million a year in yeshiva funding was revoked ([IDI, June 2025](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/59892)); the wider question of coalition money for sectoral institutions is on [the economy page](/issues/economy).

## What Israelis think

Among non-Haredi Jews, support for drafting Haredim rose from 67% in January 2024 to 84.5% in November 2024, and among national-religious respondents it doubled, from 36.5% to 72.5% ([IDI, December 2024](https://en.idi.org.il/press-releases/57753)). In the same survey, 74.5% of Likud voters and 79% of Religious Zionism voters supported it ([Times of Israel, December 2024](https://www.timesofisrael.com/survey-public-support-for-conscripting-ultra-orthodox-has-surged-throughout-war/)). Haredim were the exception: 61% wanted the current exemption kept and 20% supported some form of enlistment ([IDI, December 2024](https://en.idi.org.il/press-releases/57753)).

The burden behind that shift shows in reserve service. In 2025, 4.3% of religious men aged 21–44 were on reserve duty in an average month, against 1.7% of secular men and 0.3% of Haredi men ([IDI, August 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/65427)).

Agreement that Haredim should serve splits on who and how. In November 2025, Jewish Israelis divided between drafting everyone except outstanding yeshiva students (49.5%) and drafting only those not really studying (31%); about 75% of Haredim preferred the current system ([IDI, November 2025](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/62072)).

<Chart id="haredi-draft.who-drafted" />

In July 2026, 46% of all Israelis said more pressure should be applied to enlist Haredi men, 31% conscription only with the agreement of Haredi leaders, and 11% called the effort futile. Likud voters preferred agreement, 75%; center and left voters preferred sanctions, 73% and 79%. Among Haredim, 45% chose agreement and 15% sanctions ([JPPI via Jerusalem Post, July 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-902297)). JPPI's published write-ups do not give the sample size. Questions differ across surveys: in June 2025, 41% of Jews chose universal conscription when offered several models, while 89% wanted the status quo changed ([INSS, June 2025](https://www.inss.org.il/publication/idf-conscription/)).

The issue moves some votes: 13.7% of Jews named a party's position on conscription as the biggest influence on their vote ([IDI, September 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/media/32364/august-voice-index-data.pdf)).

## What the parties say

Most parties answered Ynet's September 2026 questionnaire on the draft; Likud declined. The two Haredi parties gave different answers on yeshiva dropouts.

## Misreadings

**It is not a church-state fight.** An American reader may hear "religious exemption" and think of the First Amendment. The Israeli complaint is that one group does not carry a duty almost everyone else carries. National-religious Israelis, the most observant Zionists, became among the strongest supporters of drafting Haredim during 2024 ([IDI, December 2024](https://en.idi.org.il/press-releases/57753)), and religious men carry the highest reserve load ([IDI, August 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/65427)).

**Haredim do serve, in growing numbers.** At least 4,000 enlisted in 2025–26, more than double the pre-war level ([Times of Israel, July 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/idf-chief-tells-haredi-commanders-army-must-grow-as-data-shows-uptick-in-enlistment/)), though that is "only slightly more than one-quarter of the estimated 14,000 haredim who reach draft age each year" ([Jerusalem Post, July 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-904035)). Few of the men who received draft orders reported: in the 2025 draft year, 701 of 15,938 whose dates had passed ([IDI, January 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/63168)).

**The Haredi parties are not one bloc.** Shas accepted that dropouts can no longer be exempt; United Torah Judaism opposed drafting them ([Ynet, September 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/sjph72wyzx)). Only 28% of Haredim give their political leadership high marks ([IDI, August 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/65364)). More on the community is on [Haredim](/communities/haredim).

**The draft does not divide right from left.** People of Israel, which still recommends Netanyahu, will not join a government that has not passed a draft law ([Jerusalem Post, September 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-election-2026/article-907373)); Yisrael Beiteinu wants "no exemptions, no quotas and no deals" ([Ynet, September 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/sjph72wyzx)). Likud voters back drafting Haredim but prefer to do it by agreement ([JPPI via Jerusalem Post, July 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-902297)).

**The election was not called months early.** The draft ended the coalition, but only after it had become "the first Israeli government in decades to complete a full four-year term" ([JNS, July 2026](https://www.jns.org/news/israel-news/knesset-votes-to-dissolve-formally-sets-oct-27-election-date)).

**American communities are part of the story.** When the 2024 funding cut took effect, Rabbi Dov Landau, a dean of the Slabodka Yeshiva in Bnei Brak, "directly appealed to Haredim in the United States" for help, the Walla news site reported ([Times of Israel, April 2024](https://www.timesofisrael.com/the-high-courts-yeshiva-funding-ruling-goes-into-effect-today-what-does-it-mean/)).
