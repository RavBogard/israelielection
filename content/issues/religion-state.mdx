## What is at stake

Israel has no civil marriage. "Israel does not allow civil marriage, and Jewish marriages conducted in Israel must be sanctioned by Orthodox rabbis" ([Pew Research Center, March 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/israels-religiously-divided-society/)). All marriages must go through religious institutions, "such as the Chief Rabbinate for Jews" ([Times of Israel, March 7, 2023](https://www.timesofisrael.com/israel-must-recognize-online-marriages-conducted-via-utah-supreme-court-rules/)). Couples who cannot or will not marry that way go abroad: 9,550 couples registered civil marriages performed abroad in 2019. Since March 2023 they can also marry online through Utah, after the Supreme Court ordered the state to register those marriages; about 1,200 couples had done so since 2020 ([Times of Israel, March 7, 2023](https://www.timesofisrael.com/israel-must-recognize-online-marriages-conducted-via-utah-supreme-court-rules/)).

The 25th Knesset spent its last year expanding religious authority:

- **Child support.** In November 2025, 53–38, a two-year interim law let rabbinical courts rule on child support when both parties consent ([Times of Israel, Nov 17, 2025](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-passes-law-expanding-rabbinical-courts-powers-to-arbitrate-civil-disputes/)).
- **Arbitration.** In March 2026, 65–41, rabbinical and Sharia courts gained the power to arbitrate civil money disputes when both sides sign consent ([VIN News, March 24, 2026](https://vinnews.com/2026/03/24/knesset-passes-rabbinic-courts-law-restoring-jurisdiction-over-arbitrated-monetary-matters/)).
- **Torah study.** In July 2026, 63–52, a Basic Law declared Torah study a "foundational value" of the state ([Times of Israel, July 14, 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-passes-contentious-basic-law-declaring-torah-study-a-constitutional-value/)). Its link to yeshiva exemptions is covered on the [draft page](/issues/haredi-draft).
- **Kashrut.** On July 15, 2026, 46–41, the Chief Rabbinate regained exclusive authority over kosher certification, reversing a reform that had let private Orthodox bodies certify ([Times of Israel, July 15, 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-passes-law-restoring-chief-rabbinates-monopoly-over-kosher-certification/)).
- **Gender segregation.** On July 16, 2026, 52–43, universities and colleges were allowed to run gender-segregated master's and doctoral programs ([Times of Israel, July 16, 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-passes-law-expanding-gender-segregation-in-academia/)).

Times of Israel reported that the kashrut law was part of a package in which the Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) parties got the Torah study law and a freeze on draft arrests in exchange for backing other coalition bills. When the arbitration law passed, opposition leader Yair Lapid said this:

Money is the other half. The 2026 budget, passed 62–55 on March 30, raised funding for Haredi educational institutions from NIS 4.1 billion to NIS 5.17 billion ([Times of Israel, March 30, 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-approves-2026-budget-israels-largest-ever-sending-billions-to-haredi-institutions/)). Separately, on March 10 the cabinet approved more than NIS 5 billion in coalition money for sectoral institutions. By a Berl Katznelson Center count, NIS 2.2 billion of it went to the Haredi community, including NIS 1.56 billion for yeshivas and kollels ([Times of Israel, March 10, 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/cabinet-okays-1-6-billion-in-coalition-funds-including-715-million-for-ultra-orthodox/)). That money is covered on the [economy page](/issues/economy).

## What Israelis think

Israeli Jews sort themselves on a scale of observance, not by denomination. In Central Bureau of Statistics data for 2025, Jews aged 20 and over defined themselves as 39.6% secular, 21.8% traditional but not particularly religious, 14.0% traditional-religious, 12.4% religious and 11.5% Haredi ([CBS via VIN News, Sept 2026](https://vinnews.com/2026/09/09/israel-enters-5787-with-population-growth-high-birthrate-and-rising-emigration/)).

In JPPI's May 2026 survey, 59% of Jewish respondents supported "having an option for civil marriage in Israel" ([JPPI, May 2026](https://jppi.org.il/en/%D7%9E%D7%93%D7%93-jppi-%D7%9C%D7%97%D7%91%D7%A8%D7%94-%D7%94%D7%99%D7%A9%D7%A8%D7%90%D7%9C%D7%99%D7%AA-%D7%9E%D7%90%D7%99-2026/)). A separate follow-up question asked about civil marriage that "would also include the possibility of marriages between Jews and non-Jews." On that question, Jewish support was 46%. Among traditional, not religious respondents it was 36%, against 54% on the general question.

Shabbat transport shows a similar split. Among Jewish respondents, support for public transport on Shabbat everywhere except heavily religious areas ranged from 67.1% to 73.8% across surveys from April 2022 to March 2025, and the report says it "gradually increased." Just over half of right-wing respondents (51.7%) supported it in March 2025 ([Reichman University, Aug 2025](https://www.runi.ac.il/media/53mmwyif/transportation_on_shabbat.pdf)).

Why does policy move against these majorities? The most recent breakdown found is from 2019. That August, asked which issues would decide their vote, 36.7% of Jews named economic and social issues and 36.2% security and foreign affairs; 15.5% called religion and state "extremely important." Among Haredim, 67.5% regarded it as of paramount importance ([IDI, Aug 2019](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/28411)). No 2026 survey breaks this down by group.

On the Western Wall and kashrut, no survey since 2019 was found. In that IDI survey, 51.5% of Jews supported egalitarian prayer in a separate area of the Wall, and 63% favored ending the Rabbinate's exclusive authority over kashrut ([IDI, Aug 2019](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/28411)).

## Misreadings

**"Reform versus Orthodox" is the American fault line, not the Israeli one.** In Pew's 2014–15 survey, 3% of Israeli Jews identified as Reform and 2% as Conservative, against 35% and 18% of American Jews in Pew's 2013 U.S. survey ([Pew Research Center, March 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2016/03/08/comparisons-between-jews-in-israel-and-the-u-s/)). Half of Israeli Jews (50%) named Orthodoxy as the stream they identify with, and 41% named none. The Israeli divide runs from secular through traditional to religious and Haredi.

**Support for civil marriage is not a demand for a wall between synagogue and state.** In a July 2025 poll of Jews commissioned by Hiddush, a group that campaigns for marriage freedom, 53% said they would choose an Orthodox marriage "if all options were legally equal" ([Jerusalem Post, Aug 2025](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-863596)). The state already funds Haredi schools and legislates the powers of rabbinical and Sharia courts, as the laws above show. The argument is over who controls religious institutions and how much they get, not whether the state is involved at all.

**Diaspora causes rank low with Israeli voters.** The Western Wall dominates American Jewish coverage. In Israel, in the last measure found (IDI, 2019), 15.5% of Jews called religion and state "extremely important" to their vote, and egalitarian prayer at the Wall had bare-majority support.

**Majorities do not make policy here.** Majorities of Jews back civil marriage and Shabbat buses, yet the 25th Knesset expanded religious authority. The IDI figures above show who puts these issues first: most Haredim, and few other Jews. The Torah study and kashrut laws were part of the coalition deal described above.

**It is not only a Jewish issue.** The 2026 arbitration law covers Sharia courts as well as rabbinical ones. Arab respondents to JPPI's May 2026 survey opposed a civil marriage option, 46% to 39%. For more, see [Palestinian citizens of Israel](/communities/palestinian-citizens).
