## What is at stake

Israel has no single written constitution. In 1950 the Knesset chose to write separate "Basic Laws" instead, to be combined later; that has not happened ([Knesset, Basic Laws](https://main.knesset.gov.il/en/activity/pages/basiclaws.aspx)). Many can be passed or changed by a simple majority. Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty passed in 1992 by 32 votes to 21 ([IDI, Feb 2023](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/47916)).

The Knesset is the only body with the power to legislate, and the government serves by virtue of its confidence ([Knesset](https://main.knesset.gov.il/EN/About/Pages/BranchesOfGovernment.aspx)). There is no second chamber. "In Israel, the Supreme Court is the only restraint on the power of the political majority," the Israel Democracy Institute wrote in 2022 ([IDI](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/46387)). Zehut, a party on the right, says the court "has accumulated powers that were never explicitly granted by legislation" ([Ynet, Sept 22, 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/hkcjtk1cme)).

In the 1995 Bank Mizrahi decision the court held that the Basic Laws have constitutional status and that it may void an ordinary law that infringes them. In that ruling Supreme Court President Aharon Barak wrote that "the constitutional revolution occurred in the Knesset in March 1992" ([Supreme Court ruling, Nov 9, 1995](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/united-mizrahi-bank-v-migdal-cooperative-village)).

In January 2023 Justice Minister Yariv Levin announced a judicial overhaul. Its parts included proposals to bar courts from voiding government decisions as "unreasonable"; give the government a built-in majority on the committee that picks judges; curb judicial review of laws; let 61 Knesset members re-enact a law the court struck down; and let ministers appoint their own legal advisers ([Times of Israel, Jan 4, 2023](https://www.timesofisrael.com/justice-minister-unveils-plan-to-shackle-the-high-court-overhaul-israels-judiciary/)). After mass protests, Netanyahu paused it on March 27, 2023 ([JTA](https://www.jta.org/2023/03/27/israel/there-must-not-be-a-civil-war-netanyahu-gives-in-to-calls-to-postpone-judiciary-reform-in-significant-victory-for-protesters)). Only the reasonableness piece passed, on July 24, 2023 ([Globes](https://en.globes.co.il/en/article-knesset-enacts-law-to-abolish-reasonableness-standard-1001453127)). On January 1, 2024 a full 15-justice panel struck it down 8–7, and 13 of the 15 held that the court may review Basic Laws ([Times of Israel, Jan 1, 2024](https://www.timesofisrael.com/in-historic-ruling-high-court-strikes-down-key-judicial-overhaul-legislation/)).

In late March 2025 the Knesset passed, with 67 votes, a law changing the selection committee. It removes the Israel Bar Association's two seats and lets Supreme Court justices be appointed without the sitting justices' consent. It takes effect when the next Knesset convenes, after the October 27 election ([Times of Israel, Mar 27, 2025](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-passes-law-greatly-boosting-political-control-over-judicial-appointments/); [Times of Israel, June 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/regime-change-high-court-tears-into-coalition-bid-to-control-judicial-appointments/)). The Supreme Court, sitting as the High Court of Justice, heard petitions against it with all 11 sitting justices on June 21, 2026; the Times of Israel counted nine of the 11 as critical of one or more parts of it. No ruling had been found as of October 5, 2026. Four of the court's 15 seats are vacant, and six more justices reach the retirement age of 70 within three years ([JTA, Sept 18, 2026](https://www.jta.org/2026/09/18/israel/judicial-reform-2-0-how-netanyahus-plans-to-reshape-the-courts-could-affect-israels-democracy)), so the next government could fill about two-thirds of the bench.

The fight also runs through the executive's lawyers. On August 4, 2025 the government voted to fire Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara, whom it accused of thwarting its will over policy, appointments and legislation ([Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/high-court-freezes-dismissal-of-attorney-general-after-government-votes-to-fire-her/)); on December 14, 2025 a seven-justice panel, conservatives included, annulled the firing 7–0 ([Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/high-court-annuls-firing-of-attorney-general-cabinet-ministers-urge-defiance-of-ruling/)). A bill to split the attorney general's job passed a first reading 65–47 on June 2, 2026. In that version, the Jerusalem Post reported, a new government-appointed prosecutor general "would have the authority to reconsider the existing indictments against Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu"; Deputy Attorney General Gil Limon called it "an abolition bill" ([Jerusalem Post, June 2, 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-898054)). The split was dropped. The law passed on July 15, 2026, 65–51, lets the government reject the attorney general's legal opinions and gives it control over the post's appointment and dismissal; prosecution stays with the attorney general, and the law takes effect in January 2027 ([Times of Israel, July 15, 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/coalition-passes-law-gutting-attorney-generals-powers-removing-key-check-on-government/)).

## What Israelis think

In each of three IDI surveys since 2023, more Israelis opposed the overhaul or its timing than backed it. In March 2026, 52% said it was not correct to advance it at this time and 32% said it was ([IDI, March 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/media/30641/israeli-voice-index-march-2026-eng-data.pdf)). It ranks low as a vote factor. Asked what would most influence their vote, 18% of Jewish respondents named the judicial system as a first or second factor, last of the listed factors, behind security (39%), the economy (38%), the draft (27%), religion and state (26%) and the party leader (23%) ([IDI, Sept 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)).

Jewish trust in the Supreme Court fell mostly before the overhaul: from 69% in 2003 to 41% in 2022, and 42% in May 2025 ([IDI Democracy Index 2025](https://en.idi.org.il/media/30427/the-israeli-democracy-index-2025-full-english-version.pdf)). The 2026 index, usually released in December, is not out; these are the latest figures.

<Chart id="courts.trust" />

The split runs along camp and religiosity. In May 2025, 83% of left-wing Jews trusted the court against 21.5% on the right; 66% of secular Jews against 3% of Haredim (ultra-Orthodox) (same source). IDI's own reading is that "political identity outweighs religiosity": secular Jews on the right trust the court at 42%.

<Chart id="courts.trust-groups" />

Palestinian citizens of Israel do not line up neatly with either camp. In January 2025, 79% of Arab respondents opposed the overhaul, against 50.5% of Jews ([IDI, Jan 2025](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/57945)). In May 2025, 55.5% of them also agreed that the court "intervenes too much" in government decisions, more than the 50% of Jews who said so ([IDI Democracy Index 2025](https://en.idi.org.il/media/30427/the-israeli-democracy-index-2025-full-english-version.pdf)). IDI's figures for "Arabs" include Druze.

## What the parties say

Coalition parties that answered Ynet's September 2026 survey want to curb the court; Religious Zionism has "one of the most extensive plans," in Ynet's words. Likud declined to answer. Outside the coalition, several lists propose new constitutional rules rather than the old arrangement: Yashar!, B'Yachad and the Reservists want a Basic Law setting rules for legislation or judicial review, and Yisrael Beiteinu wants a written constitution put to a referendum. Blue and White, an opposition list, also wants to limit the court's ability to intervene in government decisions.

## Misreadings

**It is not a fight over a culture-war docket.** American readers may expect a conservative court and a liberal opposition. Israel's fight is over the court's structure and power. Conservative justices joined the 7–0 attorney-general ruling, and the conservative justice Alex Stein was among those critical of the selection law at the June 2026 hearing ([Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/regime-change-high-court-tears-into-coalition-bid-to-control-judicial-appointments/)).

**"Judicial review" means something different.** The U.S. court reviews laws against a written constitution that is hard to amend. Israel's court reviews them against Basic Laws, many of which a simple majority can pass, and since 2024 it says it may review those Basic Laws themselves.

**Appointments work differently.** Justices are not nominated by a president and confirmed by a legislature, and they retire at 70. Under the old rules a Supreme Court appointment needed 7 of the committee's 9 votes, with three justices on it ([IDI, Dec 2022](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/46674)).

**It is not only left against right.** Trust in the court also follows religiosity, from 66% of secular Jews to 3% of Haredim (IDI, May 2025), and Palestinian citizens of Israel do not line up with either camp.

**It is not the top election issue.** Fewer than one in five Jewish voters name it as a main factor (IDI, Sept 2026).
