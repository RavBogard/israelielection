## What is at stake

Prices in Israel are rising slowly, but they are high. Inflation was 1.9% year on year in May 2026 ([Moody's via Ynet, July 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/business/article/rkqzvq4vfe)), after 2.6% in 2025 ([Bank of Israel Annual Report 2025](https://www.boi.org.il/en/communication-and-publications/regular-publications/bank-of-israel-annual-report/bank-of-israel-annual-report-2025-1/)). But food and non-alcoholic drink cost 52% more than the average across 38 OECD countries, second only to South Korea, with dairy, eggs and meat each 64% above the average ([OECD data via Times of Israel, July 2024](https://www.timesofisrael.com/food-and-beverage-prices-in-israel-52-higher-than-oecd-average-report/)). That comparison is the most recent found, and it is more than two years old.

Housing is moving in two directions. Home prices fell 1.5% in the year to May–June 2026, and prices had declined in eight of the previous 12 months, according to Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) data ([CBS via Times of Israel, September 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/housing-snapshot-september-2026-prices-inch-upward-after-sharp-decline/)). Rents rose. In the CBS release for April–May 2026, rent for new tenants was up 6.6% on a year earlier and for renewing tenants 2.6% ([CBS via Jerusalem Post, July 2026](https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/banking-and-finance/article-902755)).

The war has also left a bill. The Bank of Israel puts war-related costs for 2023–2026 at about NIS 350 billion. Public debt rose from 60.5% of GDP in early 2023 to 68.5% at the end of 2025, and the 2025 deficit was 4.7% of GDP ([Jerusalem Post, October 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-910242)). Moody's cut Israel's rating by two notches to Baa1 in September 2024 and raised its outlook to stable in February 2026 ([Times of Israel, February 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/moodys-boosts-israels-credit-outlook-to-stable-as-ceasefires-in-gaza-lebanon-hold/)), then kept the rating unchanged in July ([Ynet, July 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/business/article/rkqzvq4vfe)). S&P affirmed an A rating with a stable outlook in May ([Ynet, May 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/business/article/sj3dyricze)). Fitch rates Israel A with a negative outlook. In March 2026 Fitch warned that rising public debt and prolonged military activity could derail growth and deficit reduction, and said Israel's "fractious domestic politics" could hinder fiscal consolidation ([Reuters via KELO, March 2026](https://kelo.com/2026/03/27/fitch-affirms-israels-a-rating-with-a-negative-outlook/)).

The 2026 budget passed the Knesset 62–55 on March 30, 2026, a day before the deadline that would have triggered automatic elections. Defense received NIS 143 billion. The government approved a 3% across-the-board cut to all ministries except Defense, and funding for Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) educational institutions rose from NIS 4.1 billion to NIS 5.17 billion. A late-night amendment adding NIS 800 million for Haredi programs passed 107–4; opposition members seemed to treat it as routine and backed it automatically ([Times of Israel, March 30, 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-approves-2026-budget-israels-largest-ever-sending-billions-to-haredi-institutions/)). The fight over coalition money for sectoral institutions, which Israelis call "coalition funds", goes back further. On March 10 the cabinet approved more than NIS 5 billion of it, including NIS 1.56 billion for Torah institutions such as yeshivas and kollels; a study by the Berl Katznelson Center think tank found that NIS 2.2 billion went to the Haredi community ([Times of Israel, March 2026](https://www.timesofisrael.com/cabinet-okays-1-6-billion-in-coalition-funds-including-715-million-for-ultra-orthodox/)). Yesh Atid MK Vladimir Beliak put the total at a "record high" of NIS 6 billion ([Jerusalem Post, March 22, 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/politics-and-diplomacy/article-890836)). Neither source reconciles the two figures.

Under the budget sits a question about who works. Haredi men's employment rate was 52% in 2015 and 53% in 2025, against a government target of 65% by 2030 ([Labor Ministry report via Jerusalem Post, July 2026](https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/opinion/article-901982)). Researchers at the Israel Democracy Institute (IDI) called it "a decade of stagnation" in the integration of Haredi men and noted that over the same period there has been "rapid growth in the number of yeshiva and kollel students," men in full-time Torah study ([IDI via The Media Line, December 2025](https://themedialine.org/headlines/israel-democracy-institute-report-finds-stalled-integration-of-ultra-orthodox-men-as-community-growth-accelerates/)).

The rates in the chart come from different sources and years. Haredi women work at a far higher rate than Haredi men. Arab men's employment was 77% in early 2025, slightly below its 2023 peak of 79%, and the Taub Center expects Arab women's rate to surpass Haredi men's ([Taub Center, June 2026](https://www.taubcenter.org.il/en/pr/pon-2026/)).

Some people are also leaving Israel. In 2025, 69,300 residents left and 19,000 returned ([CBS via Ynet, December 2025](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/r1zatkmnzg)). A Tax Authority study of emigrants from 2015 to 2024 found that the number leaving from the high-tech sector grew by about 150% ([Jerusalem Post, August 2026](https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/banking-and-finance/article-904992)). High-tech was 57% of Israeli exports in the first half of 2025 ([Israel Innovation Authority, September 2025](https://innovationisrael.org.il/en/press_release/innovation-report-2025/)). The Tax Authority estimates a potential tax loss of about NIS 700 million for each new cohort of emigrants.

## What Israelis think

Among Jewish Israelis, the economy and security are close to even as reasons for a vote. In IDI's Israeli Voice Index, fielded August 31 to September 3, 2026, 38% of Jewish respondents named "economy and the high cost of living" as the first or second factor in their vote, and 39% named foreign affairs and security ([IDI, September 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)).

The economy matters more to some groups than to others. Among Jewish voters who place themselves in the Center, it ranks first, at 40.5%. On the Left, religion and state comes first (39%) and the economy second (34%). On the Right, security leads at 47%; IDI did not publish the Right's figure for the economy. Among Arab respondents the economy comes second (46%), after crime in Arab society (53.5%) ([IDI, September 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)). IDI published no breakdown of this question by religiosity, so there is no published figure for Haredim.

## Misreadings

**"It's the economy" does not work the same way.** An American reader may expect the economy to decide the vote. The IDI data show it at the top only for Center voters and Arab citizens. Many Israelis name prices as a worry and still decide their vote on security, the draft, or religion and state.

**Economic positions do not sort by bloc.** Within the Netanyahu bloc, the Likud charter, as IDI describes it, refers to "support for a competitive free market," while Shas threatened to withhold its budget vote until poor Haredi children received food-stamp support. Yisrael Beiteinu, in the opposition, is described by IDI as holding "right-wing fiscal positions" ([IDI party guide](https://en.idi.org.il/israeli-elections-and-parties/parties/yisrael-beitenu/)). In Israeli politics "right" and "left" are not, mainly, economic labels.

**The employment figure is tied to the draft.** "53% of Haredi men work" can read like a job-training problem. In Israel it is bound up with yeshiva funding and military service. The Finance Ministry's chief economist found that non-Haredi Jewish men are 36% of all employees but 83% of those absent from work for reserve duty ([Finance Ministry via Arutz Sheva, August 2026](https://www.israelnationalnews.com/news/432226)). The same figures run through the debate over the [Haredi draft](/issues/haredi-draft). See also [religion and state](/issues/religion-state).

**"Cost of living" means price levels.** With inflation under 2%, the complaint is about prices that are already high. B'Yachad, in the opposition, promises to break up food cartels; it does not target the central bank.

**A strong market and strained households can both be true.** Tel Aviv's benchmark index rose 52% in 2025, and a third of households spend more than they earn ([Time, July 2026](https://time.com/article/2026/07/13/israel-war-economy-cost-ordinary-israelis/)).
