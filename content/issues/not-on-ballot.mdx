## What is at stake

In Israel, left and right are defined mostly by positions on the conflict with the Palestinians, not by economics ([CHES-Israel expert survey, 2021–22](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13540688231218917)). Yet in the 2026 campaign almost no one argues for a Palestinian state. Of the lists running on October 27, only the two Arab-led lists, Ra'am and the Joint List, say they support a Palestinian state ([Jerusalem Post, Sept 1, 2026](https://www.jpost.com/israel-election-2026/article-907154)). The Zionist lists oppose one, say it is "not on the agenda", decline to answer, or, in the case of The Democrats, speak of "separation" ([Ynet party questionnaire, Sept 30, 2026](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/h1dpvu9cgl)).

The Knesset put this on record before the campaign began. On July 17, 2024 it passed, 68–9, a resolution stating that "the Knesset of Israel firmly opposes the establishment of a Palestinian state west of Jordan." Unlike a February 2024 resolution, which rejected only unilateral recognition, it rejected a state even as part of a negotiated settlement. Coalition parties were joined by members of Benny Gantz's National Unity; the nine votes against came from Ra'am and Hadash–Ta'al, and Yesh Atid and Labor left the chamber ([Times of Israel, July 2024](https://www.timesofisrael.com/knesset-votes-overwhelmingly-against-palestinian-statehood-days-before-pms-us-trip/)).

Since then, Knesset votes have been about annexation rather than partition. On July 23, 2025 a non-binding declaration in favor of annexing the West Bank and the Jordan Valley passed 71–13 ([Ynet, July 2025](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/bkxfw9algl)). On October 22, 2025 a bill by Noam's Avi Maoz to annex the settlement areas of the West Bank passed a preliminary reading 25–24 ([Times of Israel, Oct 22, 2025](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog_entry/rebelling-against-pm-mks-pass-preliminary-reading-of-west-bank-annexation-bill/)). The [West Bank page](/issues/west-bank) covers settlement and annexation in detail.

Abroad, the question moved the other way. By late September 2025, 157 of the 193 UN member states had recognized the State of Palestine, among them the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and France in September 2025 ([Al Jazeera, Sept 23, 2025](https://aljazeera.com/news/2025/9/23/which-are-the-150-countries-that-have-recognised-palestine-as-of-2025)). In Israel the recognitions united the Zionist parties rather than dividing them: Yair Lapid called it "a harmful step, and a reward for terror", Yair Golan called it "destructive", and Itamar Ben-Gvir called it "a prize for the murderous terrorists" ([Times of Israel, Sept 22, 2025](https://www.timesofisrael.com/israel-unites-against-hollow-palestine-recognition-but-pm-could-go-too-far/)).

When the Israel Democracy Institute asked voters to name their most important considerations, Jewish respondents chose foreign affairs and security (39%), the economy and cost of living (38%) and conscription (27%). The Palestinian issue was not offered as a separate answer ([IDI, Aug 31–Sept 3, 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)). In the campaign, Netanyahu has put the question this way:

<Quote who="Benjamin Netanyahu" role="Prime Minister, Likud" source="Jerusalem Post" url="https://www.jpost.com/israel-election-2026/article-910068" date="Sept 18, 2026">Do you want a Palestinian state? (or) do you want a Jewish state?</Quote>

## What Israelis think

In 2026, 17% of Israelis and 16% of Jewish Israelis think Israel and a Palestinian state can coexist peacefully, down from 50% of all Israelis in 2013. For the first time in Pew's polling, more than half (56%) say coexistence is not possible ([Pew, Sept 15, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/09/15/many-israelis-and-palestinians-say-they-cannot-coexist-peacefully-and-explain-why/)). Pew says the share has not changed significantly from 21% in 2025. Pew's question asks about feasibility, not preference.

In Pew's 2025 survey, 54% of Israelis on the left, 29% in the center and 7% on the right said a way can be found for Israel and a Palestinian state to coexist. Among Jewish Israelis, 25% of Hilonim (secular), 11% of Masortim (traditional) and 9% of Haredim (ultra-Orthodox) and Datiim (religious), counted together, said peaceful coexistence is possible ([Pew, Feb–March 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/06/pg_2025.06.03_israel_report.pdf)).

Tel Aviv University's Peace Index asks a different question: how far people support or oppose each of several outcomes. Among Jews, support for "the creation of an independent Palestinian state alongside the State of Israel" fell from 38% a month before October 7, 2023 to 23% in March 2026. Support for "annexation of the occupied territories and the creation of one state under Israeli rule with limited rights for Palestinians" rose from 30% to 38%, though in November 2025 half of Jews said they opposed it ([Peace Index, Nov 2025](https://en-social-sciences.tau.ac.il/sites/socsci-english.tau.ac.il/files/media_server/social/peaceindex/2025-11.pdf)). Whatever they prefer, 48% of Jews expect the current situation to continue ([Peace Index, March 2026](https://en-social-sciences.tau.ac.il/sites/socsci-english.tau.ac.il/files/media_server/social/peaceindex/2026-03.pdf)).

The answers depend on the menu. When the Institute for National Security Studies offered a single choice that included "separation from the Palestinians", separation led among Jews at 32%, level with "full annexation without granting civil rights" at 31% ([INSS, June 16–22, 2026](https://www.inss.org.il/publication/inss-survey-june-2026/)).

Palestinian citizens of Israel are far more favorable. In the March 2026 Peace Index, 71% of Arab respondents supported a Palestinian state alongside Israel, from a small subsample of 103; in the INSS survey, 57% of Arab respondents chose two states. Their own first voting concern is crime in Arab society (53.5%) ([IDI, Sept 2026](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/66177)). The [Palestinian citizens page](/communities/palestinian-citizens) has more.

## Misreadings

**The election is not a referendum on two states.** No Zionist list running on October 27 campaigns for a Palestinian state. Two of Netanyahu's leading opponents, Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar! and Naftali Bennett's B'Yachad, answered Ynet that a state is "not on the agenda" and "We oppose a Palestinian state", respectively.

**Left and right do not mean what they mean in the United States.** As the CHES survey above found, Israel's axis is the conflict. Bennett and Eisenkot oppose Netanyahu but are not on the left on this question, and the Knesset's 68 votes against a state in 2024 included centrists from Gantz's party.

**Young Israelis are not the most dovish.** In the Palestinian-Israeli Pulse of December 2022, the latest age breakdown found, 20% of Jewish Israelis aged 18–34 backed the two-state solution in general, against 47% of those over 55 ([Pulse, TAU and PSR, Dec 2022](https://resolution.tau.ac.il/sites/socsci-english.tau.ac.il/files/media_server/resolution/Summary%20Report_%20English_Joint%20Poll%2024%20Jan%202023.pdf)).

**American Jews answer differently.** Asked Pew's same question before the war, 63% of American Jews thought a way could be found for Israel and an independent Palestinian state to coexist peacefully ([Pew, fieldwork Nov 2019–June 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/05/11/u-s-jews-connections-with-and-attitudes-toward-israel/)). The comparable Israeli Jewish figure in 2026 is 16%.

<Note title="American Jews after October 7">No post-war survey of American Jews found for this page repeats the coexistence question. In February 2024 Pew asked a different one: 46% of American Jews said a two-state solution would be the best possible outcome ([Pew, Feb 13–25, 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2024/03/21/majority-in-u-s-say-israel-has-valid-reasons-for-fighting-fewer-say-the-same-about-hamas/)).</Note>

**Opposing a state is not the same as backing annexation.** Among Jews, opposition to a Palestinian state is far larger than support for any single alternative. The status quo and separation draw large shares, and the outcome Jews most often expect, the current situation, is not the one they most often prefer.
